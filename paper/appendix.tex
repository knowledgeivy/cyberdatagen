\subsection{Prompt Templates}
\label{appendix:prompts}

All prompts follow a consistent template structure with only the strategy-specific instructions varying. The general template format is:

\begin{verbatim}
You are an expert email content analyzer. Given the following
spam email, generate a semantically similar but lexically
distinct spam variant.

Instructions: [Strategy-Specific Instructions]

Original Email:
{original_spam_content}

Output Format (JSON):
{
  "subject": "generated subject line",
  "body": "generated email body"
}
\end{verbatim}

\noindent\textbf{Strategy-Specific Instructions:}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Original Strategy:} Faithfully reproduce the spam characteristics including urgency signals, persuasive elements, and social engineering tactics. Introduce natural lexical variation while maintaining semantic similarity and malicious intent.

    \item \textbf{Strong Strategy:} Amplify spam indicators by emphasizing urgency signals (limited time, immediate action required), persuasive elements (too-good-to-be-true offers, authority figures), and social engineering tactics (credential requests, fake notifications). Make the spam characteristics more explicit and prominent.

    \item \textbf{Weak Strategy:} Generate subtle spam with reduced explicit indicators. Create content that appears more legitimate while retaining underlying spam nature. Use professional language and minimize obvious urgency signals, making the spam more difficult to detect.
\end{itemize}

The \texttt{\{original\_spam\_content\}} placeholder is replaced with actual spam email text during generation, ensuring automated integration into the experimental pipeline.

\subsection{Performance Metrics Definitions}
\label{appendix:metrics}

Classification performance metrics computed on fixed out-of-sample test set, where $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, $FN$ represent confusion matrix elements (True/False Positives/Negatives):

\paragraph{F1-Score (Primary Metric):} Harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\[
\text{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot TP}{2 \cdot TP + FP + FN}
\]

\paragraph{Supporting Metrics:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy:} $(TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)$
    \item \textbf{Precision:} $TP / (TP + FP)$ - false positive control
    \item \textbf{Recall:} $TP / (TP + FN)$ - detection sensitivity
    \item \textbf{AUC-ROC:} Area under ROC curve, threshold-independent discrimination
    \item \textbf{Balanced Accuracy:} $\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{TP}{TP+FN} + \frac{TN}{TN+FP}\right)$ - class-imbalance robust
\end{itemize}

F1-score serves as primary metric due to balanced treatment of precision and recall under 10\% class imbalance.

\subsection{Mixing Strategy Comparison}
\label{appendix:mixing_strategy_comparison}

Preliminary experiments compared within-group mixing (training on synthetic data from same group) versus cross-group mixing (training on synthetic data from different group). Cross-group strategy shows consistent advantages: mean F1 improvement of +0.009 ($p < 10^{-6}$, Cohen's $d = 0.13$) across all configurations. More importantly, cross-group mixing better reflects realistic deployment scenarios where synthetic generation sources may differ temporally or distributionally from operational training data. All main experiments employ cross-group strategy based on these findings.
