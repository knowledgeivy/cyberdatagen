\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Research Motivation and Significance}

The effectiveness of machine learning-based cybersecurity defenses fundamentally depends on training data quality and quantity. However, acquiring sufficient labeled cybersecurity data confronts persistent challenges: high annotation costs requiring domain expertise, severe class imbalance where malicious samples constitute small minorities, privacy concerns preventing cross-organizational data sharing, and temporal drift as attack patterns evolve faster than dataset collection cycles. These constraints create critical bottlenecks in developing robust detection systems, particularly for emerging threat variants with limited real-world examples.

Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a transformative paradigm for addressing data scarcity in cybersecurity domains. Unlike traditional data augmentation methods operating in fixed feature spaces, LLMs generate semantically coherent text in the original input space, providing several unique capabilities:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Semantic Generation}: LLMs produce contextually appropriate content preserving linguistic coherence and domain-specific characteristics, generating samples exhibiting realistic patterns rather than artificial feature combinations.

\item \textbf{Controllable Generation}: Prompt engineering enables systematic manipulation of generation parameters, allowing specification of desired characteristics and exploration of diverse threat scenarios without collecting new real-world samples.

\item \textbf{Text-Space Augmentation}: By generating actual text rather than abstract feature vectors, LLM-produced samples support multiple downstream applications including adversarial testing, human review, and transfer learning across detection systems.

\item \textbf{Rapid Adaptability}: LLM generation requires no manual annotation once initial prompts are designed, enabling adaptation to emerging patterns through prompt modifications rather than expensive data collection campaigns.
\end{itemize}

Despite these theoretical advantages, the practical utility of LLM-generated synthetic data for cybersecurity training remains empirically underexplored. Critical questions persist: Does synthetic data improve classification performance? How does effectiveness scale with varying synthetic proportions? Most fundamentally, \emph{do LLMs provide advantages over established augmentation methods?} This research addresses these questions through rigorous empirical comparison of neural text generation against traditional feature-space augmentation, establishing evidence-based guidance for synthetic data deployment in operational systems.

\subsection{Data Augmentation Paradigms: Text-Space vs Feature-Space}

To contextualize our LLM-based approach, we establish fundamental distinctions between neural text generation and traditional augmentation methods, focusing on SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) as a widely-adopted baseline for imbalanced learning tasks.

\subsubsection{Feature-Space Augmentation: SMOTE Baseline}

SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} addresses class imbalance through feature-space interpolation. After transforming text documents to TF-IDF feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, SMOTE generates synthetic minority-class samples through linear interpolation between each sample and its $k$ nearest minority-class neighbors:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{synth}}^{\text{SMOTE}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda \cdot (\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i), \quad \lambda \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1), \quad \mathbf{x}_j \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ denotes the $k$ nearest neighbors. This convex-hull interpolation ensures all synthetic samples lie within the feature-space boundaries defined by real minority samples.

\textbf{Critical Limitation:} The synthetic vector exists solely in feature space with \emph{no corresponding text representation}. Due to non-invertibility of feature extraction, these samples serve only immediate classification purposes and cannot support applications requiring actual text content. Complete SMOTE formalization is provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:smote_details}.

\subsubsection{Text-Space Generation: LLM Approach}

In contrast, LLM-based augmentation operates in the original text space. Given a real minority-class example $\text{doc}_i$, LLMs generate synthetic text through prompt-guided neural generation:

\begin{equation}
\text{doc}_{\text{synth}}^{\text{LLM}} = \text{LLM}(\text{prompt}(\text{doc}_i, \text{strategy}) \mid \Theta), \quad \mathbf{x}_{\text{synth}}^{\text{LLM}} = \phi(\text{doc}_{\text{synth}}^{\text{LLM}})
\end{equation}

where $\Theta$ represents learned parameters, strategy controls generation characteristics, and $\phi(\cdot)$ denotes feature extraction. \textbf{Key Distinction:} The synthetic sample exists as interpretable text \emph{before} vectorization, enabling human inspection, semantic validation, and reuse across multiple tasks rather than serving only immediate classification.

\subsection{Framework Overview}

Our methodology presents a systematic framework for rigorously evaluating synthetic spam data across both neural text generation (LLMs) and traditional feature-space augmentation (SMOTE) paradigms through large-scale replication and comprehensive statistical analysis. The framework addresses fundamental challenges in synthetic cybersecurity data assessment: quantifying quality-performance relationships across augmentation methods, establishing statistical confidence in observed effects, and providing actionable guidance for operational deployment. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}, our approach integrates controlled generation, systematic mixing, and rigorous statistical evaluation to produce empirically grounded insights into synthetic data effectiveness while enabling direct comparison between text-space and feature-space augmentation approaches.

Our framework encompasses four primary stages that collectively enable rigorous assessment of synthetic spam data utility:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stratified Data Preparation}: Systematic partitioning of real spam corpus into independent experimental groups using stratified random sampling, ensuring statistical independence while maintaining representation of diverse spam patterns across all groups.

\item \textbf{Controlled Synthetic Generation}: Application of multiple generation paradigms (LLM text-space generation via prompt engineering, SMOTE feature-space interpolation) to systematically explore different augmentation approaches, enabling controlled investigation of paradigm-specific characteristics on data quality and downstream performance.

\item \textbf{Systematic Mixing and Training}: Implementation of two fundamental mixing strategies (within-group and cross-group) across varying synthetic proportions, combined with multiple classifier architectures to comprehensively evaluate synthetic data utility under diverse conditions.

\item \textbf{Statistical Evaluation and Analysis}: Comprehensive assessment employing replicated measurements, hypothesis testing with multiple comparison correction, effect size quantification, and sensitivity analysis to establish statistically rigorous conclusions about synthetic data effectiveness.
\end{itemize}

This sequential approach ensures that each stage builds upon the previous one while maintaining clear separation of concerns, enabling systematic analysis of each component's contribution to overall synthetic data quality and utility.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations and Design Rationale}

The framework is grounded in four fundamental principles that address key challenges in synthetic cybersecurity data evaluation.

\subsubsection{Statistical Replication for Robust Inference}

Our adoption of replicated experimental design reflects statistical power analysis requirements for detecting practically meaningful effects. The required sample size $R$ (number of independent groups) is determined through power analysis:
\begin{equation}
\text{Power}(R, \delta, \alpha) = P(\text{reject } H_0 | H_1 \text{ true}) = f(R, \delta, \alpha, \sigma)
\end{equation}
where $\delta$ is the minimum detectable effect size, $\alpha$ is the significance level, and $\sigma$ is the expected standard deviation. This design choice transforms synthetic data evaluation from exploratory single-point estimation to rigorous hypothesis testing with quantified uncertainty, enabling confident conclusions about synthetic data effectiveness across operational variability. The specific value of $R$ for our experiments is determined in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.

The replication approach addresses a critical limitation in existing synthetic data research, where single-trial experiments cannot distinguish genuine treatment effects from random variation. By observing performance across $R$ independent groups, we can quantify both central tendency (mean performance) and dispersion (standard deviation, confidence intervals), providing complete characterization of synthetic data impact rather than point estimates that may be unrepresentative.

\subsubsection{Controlled Prompt Engineering for Systematic Exploration}

The three-strategy prompt design (original, strong, weak) reflects our hypothesis that generation instructions systematically influence synthetic data characteristics along an intensity continuum. Rather than arbitrary prompt selection, we deliberately vary instruction emphasis on spam features to explore the generation parameter space systematically. This approach enables us to test whether explicit amplification of malicious characteristics (strong prompts) improves training utility compared to faithful reproduction (original) or subtle generation (weak prompts).

The controlled variation design follows principles from experimental psychology and human-computer interaction, where systematic manipulation of independent variables enables causal inference about their effects. By varying only prompt intensity while holding other factors constant, we isolate the contribution of generation instructions to downstream classifier performance.

\subsubsection{Distribution Matching Through Mixing Strategies}

Our within-group versus cross-group mixing strategies address fundamental questions about synthetic data generalization. The within-group strategy tests synthetic data effectiveness under ideal conditions where generated samples closely match the target training distribution, representing best-case scenarios where synthetic generation successfully captures local distributional characteristics.

The cross-group strategy deliberately introduces distributional mismatch by sourcing synthetic samples from different spam groups, testing whether synthetic data provides value through diversity and coverage rather than precise distribution matching. This design enables us to distinguish between two competing hypotheses about synthetic data mechanisms: (1) effectiveness requires close distribution matching, versus (2) effectiveness derives from semantic diversity that improves generalization. Real-world deployment often cannot guarantee precise distribution matching, making cross-group performance critical for assessing practical utility.

\subsubsection{Multi-Architecture Evaluation for Generalizability}

The inclusion of both Support Vector Machine (margin-based) and Random Forest (ensemble-based) classifiers addresses the concern that synthetic data effectiveness may be classifier-specific. Different classification architectures make fundamentally different assumptions about decision boundaries and data characteristics. SVMs construct optimal separating hyperplanes based on support vectors, potentially exhibiting sensitivity to distributional shifts in synthetic data. Random Forests aggregate multiple weak learners, potentially showing robustness to individual sample quality issues through ensemble averaging.

By evaluating synthetic data impact across architecturally distinct classifiers, we can distinguish between universal synthetic data properties and architecture-specific sensitivities, providing guidance for selecting appropriate classifiers when incorporating synthetic training data.

\subsection{Methodological Innovation}

The framework introduces several methodological innovations that advance the state of synthetic data evaluation in cybersecurity contexts.

\subsubsection{Large-Scale Replication for Statistical Rigor}

Our $R = 20$ replication design represents, to our knowledge, the first application of large-scale statistical replication to synthetic cybersecurity data evaluation. Prior work typically reports single-trial results or limited replication ($R \leq 5$), providing insufficient statistical power for confident inference. Our design enables:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis testing with multiplicity control}: Application of False Discovery Rate correction to control type I error inflation across multiple comparisons, ensuring reported significant effects are unlikely to be spurious.

    \item \textbf{Effect size quantification}: Calculation of Cohen's $d$ effect sizes to distinguish between statistically significant but practically negligible effects versus substantial impacts that warrant operational consideration.

    \item \textbf{Confidence interval estimation}: Precise quantification of uncertainty in performance estimates, enabling risk-aware decision making about synthetic data deployment.

    \item \textbf{Sensitivity analysis}: Regression modeling of performance as a function of synthetic proportion, quantifying degradation rates and enabling performance prediction for arbitrary mixing ratios.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Systematic Mixing Strategy Investigation}

The explicit comparison of within-group versus cross-group mixing strategies addresses a gap in existing research, which typically employs a single mixing approach without investigating alternatives. Our systematic investigation enables empirical answers to critical deployment questions: Can synthetic data from one source effectively augment training data from another source? Does precise distribution matching matter, or is semantic diversity sufficient? These questions directly impact operational decisions about synthetic data sourcing, transfer learning, and temporal adaptation.

\subsubsection{Comprehensive Performance Degradation Characterization}

Our sensitivity analysis framework, modeling classifier performance as a function of synthetic proportion through linear regression, provides quantitative degradation metrics that enable cost-benefit analysis. The regression slope ($\beta_1$) quantifies expected performance change per percentage point increase in synthetic data, while $R^2$ indicates whether degradation patterns are linear (enabling simple prediction) or non-linear (requiring more complex modeling). This framework transforms qualitative observations about synthetic data impact into quantitative relationships suitable for operational decision support.

\subsubsection{Systematic Paradigm Comparison}

Our framework establishes, to our knowledge, the first rigorous empirical comparison between neural text-space generation (LLMs) and traditional feature-space interpolation (SMOTE) for cybersecurity data augmentation. This paradigm comparison addresses a fundamental question: do neural methods' text interpretability and generation flexibility justify their computational costs compared to established feature-space approaches? By applying identical experimental protocols, statistical analyses, and evaluation metrics across both paradigms, we enable direct performance and reliability comparisons that isolate paradigm-specific characteristics from experimental artifacts. This systematic comparison provides evidence-based guidance for practitioners choosing between augmentation approaches based on operational requirements, computational constraints, and interpretability needs.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics Framework}

Our methodology employs a comprehensive metrics suite that captures synthetic data effectiveness from multiple perspectives, enabling both immediate performance assessment and long-term optimization guidance.

\subsubsection{Classification Performance Metrics}

Comprehensive evaluation requires multiple metrics capturing different performance aspects. We employ a balanced metrics suite combining:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Threshold-dependent metrics}: Measures evaluated at fixed decision thresholds, including precision (false positive control), recall (false negative control), and their harmonic mean F1-score (balanced assessment under class imbalance).

\item \textbf{Threshold-independent metrics}: Measures aggregating performance across all decision thresholds, such as AUC-ROC (overall discrimination ability) and AUC-PR (performance under severe imbalance).

\item \textbf{Balanced measures}: Metrics designed to handle class imbalance through normalization or comprehensive confusion matrix consideration, providing robust assessment when class distributions are skewed.
\end{itemize}

This multi-faceted approach ensures that synthetic data effectiveness is evaluated comprehensively rather than through single-aspect assessment. The specific metrics employed and their mathematical definitions are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:experiment} and Appendix~\ref{appendix:metrics} respectively.

\subsubsection{Statistical Analysis Metrics}

Beyond classification performance, our framework employs rigorous statistical metrics to quantify confidence, effect magnitude, and degradation patterns. The replicated design with $R = 20$ independent groups enables:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Descriptive Statistics}: Mean and standard deviation quantifying expected performance and consistency across operational variability, providing complete distributional characterization rather than single-point estimates

\item \textbf{Confidence Intervals}: t-distribution-based intervals quantifying estimation uncertainty for all performance metrics, with narrow intervals indicating precise estimates and wide intervals suggesting high variability requiring cautious interpretation

\item \textbf{Hypothesis Testing}: Paired comparisons testing each synthetic ratio against the 0\% baseline, with Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries across multiple simultaneous tests

\item \textbf{Effect Sizes}: Cohen's $d$ standardized effect sizes distinguishing statistical significance from practical importance, following conventional guidelines for negligible, small, medium, and large effects

\item \textbf{Sensitivity Analysis}: Linear regression modeling performance as a function of synthetic proportion, with regression slope ($\beta_1$) quantifying expected degradation rate and $R^2$ indicating pattern linearity
\end{itemize}

Complete mathematical formulations for all statistical metrics are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:statistics}. Specific parameter values (e.g., confidence levels, significance thresholds) are determined in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.

\subsection{Quality Assurance and Validation}

Our methodology incorporates comprehensive quality assurance mechanisms ensuring reliable and reproducible results:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sampling Quality Control}: Stratified sampling maintaining consistent 10\% spam ratio across all 20 independent groups, with post-sampling validation confirming balanced spam characteristic distributions

    \item \textbf{Generation Quality Verification}: All LLM-generated samples validated for successful parsing and completeness, with failed generations logged and excluded from analysis

    \item \textbf{Experimental Reproducibility}: Fixed random seeds for all stochastic processes, consistent test set across all experimental conditions, standardized hyperparameters isolating synthetic data impact, and version-controlled code/prompts/analysis scripts

    \item \textbf{Statistical Validity}: Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality assumptions with non-parametric alternatives when violated, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction controlling false discoveries across multiple comparisons, and Cohen's $d$ effect sizes distinguishing statistical from practical significance
\end{itemize}

Complete quality assurance specifications and validation procedures are documented in Appendix~\ref{appendix:quality_control}.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations and Scope}

While our framework provides rigorous evaluation of synthetic spam data, several limitations define the scope of our conclusions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feature Representation}: Our evaluation employs TF-IDF feature representation, which captures lexical patterns but may miss deeper semantic or structural characteristics. Deep learning classifiers operating on embeddings might exhibit different sensitivity profiles.

    \item \textbf{Dataset Specificity}: Results are based on the CEAS-08 spam corpus, which represents spam patterns from a specific time period and threat landscape. Generalization to other spam datasets or evolving spam tactics requires empirical validation.

    \item \textbf{Class Imbalance Range}: Our fixed 10\% spam ratio reflects common operational conditions but does not explore the full spectrum of possible imbalance scenarios. More extreme imbalances may exhibit different synthetic data effectiveness patterns.

    \item \textbf{LLM Selection}: We employ GPT-4.1-mini as our generation engine. Different LLMs with varying capabilities may produce synthetic data with different quality characteristics and performance impacts.
\end{itemize}

These limitations define boundary conditions for our findings and suggest directions for future research extending the framework to additional datasets, classifiers, and generation models.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

By integrating large-scale replication, systematic paradigm comparison, controlled mixing strategies, and comprehensive statistical analysis, our methodology establishes a rigorous framework for synthetic cybersecurity data evaluation. The framework's emphasis on statistical power, effect size quantification, paradigm-level comparison, and degradation characterization moves synthetic data research beyond exploratory single-trial evaluations toward confirmatory hypothesis testing with quantified uncertainty across augmentation approaches.

The systematic comparison between text-space neural generation and feature-space interpolation provides a methodological template for evaluating emerging augmentation techniques against established baselines, enabling researchers to assess whether novel methods offer substantive improvements or merely superficial differences. This comparative foundation prevents premature adoption of complex neural approaches when simpler traditional methods suffice, while identifying scenarios where neural generation's unique capabilities (text interpretability, semantic control) justify additional complexity and cost.

This methodological foundation enables evidence-based decision making about synthetic data deployment in operational cybersecurity systems, providing practitioners with quantitative guidance for selecting between augmentation paradigms, choosing mixing ratios, and predicting performance impacts. The framework's modular design facilitates adaptation to other cybersecurity domains beyond spam detection, potentially advancing synthetic data research across malware analysis, intrusion detection, and threat intelligence applications while establishing evaluation standards for comparing traditional and neural augmentation approaches.
