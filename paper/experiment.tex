\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiment}

This section describes our comprehensive experimental framework designed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness and limitations of LLM-generated synthetic spam data for training spam classifiers. Our design employs a multi-factorial approach with systematic replication to ensure statistical reliability and generalizability of findings.

\subsection{Dataset and Sampling Strategy}

\subsubsection{Source Dataset}

We conduct our experiments using the CEAS-08 spam email dataset~\cite{ceas08}, which contains 39,154 real-world email samples with 21,842 labeled as spam (55.8\%) and 17,312 as legitimate (44.2\%). This dataset provides a substantial corpus of authentic spam emails that capture diverse spam patterns and tactics observed in real-world email systems.

\subsubsection{Stratified Group Sampling}

Following the replicated design framework established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, we employ stratified random sampling to partition the spam corpus into $R = 20$ independent groups. This sample size is determined through power analysis (Equation~3.1 in Methodology): with $R = 20$, we achieve statistical power exceeding 80\% for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's $d \geq 0.5$) at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, assuming typical performance variance $\sigma \approx 0.03$ observed in preliminary experiments.

Each group contains $N = 1,000$ samples with a fixed spam ratio of 10\% (100 spam, 900 legitimate), reflecting realistic class imbalance commonly observed in operational email filtering systems. The stratification process ensures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Statistical independence}: Each group is sampled without replacement, creating 20 statistically independent experimental units.
    \item \textbf{Distributional balance}: Stratified sampling maintains representation of diverse spam patterns across all groups.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Train-Test Split}

We employ a fixed out-of-sample test set to ensure consistent evaluation across all experimental conditions. The test set, comprising 20\% of the total dataset, is held out before any experimental manipulation and remains constant across all configurations. This approach eliminates test set variance as a confounding factor and enables fair comparison across different synthetic data strategies and ratios.

The training data from each group is further used to construct experimental datasets with varying synthetic data proportions, while the test set remains pure real-world data throughout all experiments.

\subsection{Synthetic Data Generation}

\subsubsection{LLM Configuration}

We employ two state-of-the-art large language models to evaluate the robustness and generalizability of LLM-based synthetic data generation across different model architectures:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini} (OpenAI): A compact yet capable model optimized for efficiency while maintaining strong generation quality. Configuration: temperature $= 0.7$, max tokens $= 1{,}000$, top-p $= 1.0$, no frequency or presence penalties.

    \item \textbf{Claude-3.5-Haiku} (Anthropic): A fast, cost-effective model designed for high-throughput applications with competitive performance. Configuration: temperature $= 0.7$, max tokens $= 1{,}000$, standard sampling parameters.
\end{enumerate}

Both models use identical temperature settings and generation parameters to ensure fair comparison. The selection of these two models provides architectural diversity (OpenAI GPT vs Anthropic Claude) while maintaining practical feasibility for large-scale generation. Complete configuration details are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:config}, Table~\ref{tab:llm_config}.

\subsubsection{Prompt Engineering Strategies}

To investigate the impact of generation instructions on synthetic data quality, we design three distinct prompt strategies that vary in their guidance intensity:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Original Prompt}: Instructs the LLM to rewrite spam emails while preserving malicious intent and semantic content. This serves as our baseline generation approach, emphasizing faithful reproduction of spam characteristics.

    \item \textbf{Strong Prompt}: Encourages amplification of spam features by explicitly requesting emphasis on malicious characteristics and obvious spam indicators. This strategy tests whether more explicit spam signals improve classifier training.

    \item \textbf{Weak Prompt}: Promotes subtle spam generation by requesting less obvious malicious content while maintaining spam intent. This strategy explores whether more legitimate-appearing spam variants provide training value.
\end{enumerate}

All prompts follow a consistent template structure with systematic instructions and JSON-formatted output requirements to ensure parsing reliability. Complete prompt templates are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:prompts}.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Data Mixing Strategies}

We implement two fundamental mixing strategies to evaluate different synthetic data deployment scenarios:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Within-Group Strategy}: For each group $i$, synthetic spam is generated from real spam samples \emph{within the same group}. The training set combines real spam from group $i$ with synthetic spam generated from group $i$'s real samples. This strategy tests the effectiveness of synthetic data when it closely matches the target distribution.

    Mathematical formulation:
    \[
    \text{Training}_i = \text{Real}_i^{(1-\theta)} \cup \text{Synthetic}_{i}^{(\theta)}
    \]
    where $\theta$ is the synthetic proportion and synthetic samples are generated from $\text{Real}_i$.

    \item \textbf{Cross-Group Strategy}: For each group $i$, synthetic spam is generated from real spam samples in \emph{different groups} ($j \neq i$). This strategy tests the generalization capability of synthetic data when it does not precisely match the target distribution.

    Mathematical formulation:
    \[
    \text{Training}_i = \text{Real}_i^{(1-\theta)} \cup \text{Synthetic}_{j \neq i}^{(\theta)}
    \]
\end{enumerate}

These two strategies enable us to distinguish between in-distribution synthetic data effectiveness (within-group) and cross-distribution generalization (cross-group), providing insights into the robustness and transfer learning properties of LLM-generated spam.

\subsubsection{SMOTE Baseline Configuration}

To contextualize the performance and utility of LLM-based text generation, we implement SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique)~\cite{chawla2002smote} as a traditional feature-space augmentation baseline. As formalized in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, SMOTE operates fundamentally differently from LLMs by generating synthetic samples through linear interpolation in the vectorized feature space rather than text space.

\textbf{Implementation Details}: We apply SMOTE using the \texttt{imblearn} library~\cite{lemaitre2017imbalanced} with the following configuration:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interpolation method}: Standard SMOTE with $k=5$ nearest neighbors
    \item \textbf{Sampling strategy}: Oversample minority class (spam) only to achieve target synthetic ratios
    \item \textbf{Random seed}: Fixed at 42 for reproducibility across all groups
    \item \textbf{Feature space}: Operates on TF-IDF vectors (identical to LLM pipeline)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Experimental Design}: SMOTE baseline experiments follow an analogous structure to LLM experiments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mixing strategies}: Both within-group and cross-group configurations
    \item \textbf{Synthetic ratios}: Identical 11 levels (0\%, 10\%, ..., 100\%)
    \item \textbf{Replication}: Same 20 independent groups
    \item \textbf{Evaluation}: Identical test set and performance metrics
\end{itemize}

This parallel experimental design enables direct performance comparison while highlighting the fundamental architectural distinction: SMOTE generates abstract feature vectors without corresponding text, whereas LLMs generate interpretable text samples that are subsequently vectorized.

\subsection{Experimental Factors and Design}

Our comprehensive experimental design encompasses two complementary evaluation tracks that enable rigorous assessment of both LLM-based and traditional augmentation approaches.

\subsubsection{LLM Experiment Track}

The LLM experiments follow a full-factorial design with four primary factors:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model}: 2 levels (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku)
    \item \textbf{Prompt Strategy}: 3 levels (Original, Strong, Weak)
    \item \textbf{Mixing Strategy}: 2 levels (Within-Group, Cross-Group)
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Ratio} ($\theta$): 11 levels (0\%, 10\%, 20\%, ..., 100\%)
\end{enumerate}

This yields $2 \times 3 \times 2 \times 11 = 132$ unique experimental configurations. Each configuration is replicated across $R = 20$ independent groups and evaluated with 2 classifiers (SVM, Random Forest), resulting in $132 \times 20 \times 2 = 5,280$ training-evaluation units.

\subsubsection{SMOTE Baseline Track}

The SMOTE experiments follow a simplified factorial design with three factors:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mixing Strategy}: 2 levels (Within-Group, Cross-Group)
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Ratio} ($\theta$): 11 levels (0\%, 10\%, 20\%, ..., 100\%)
    \item \textbf{Classifier}: 2 levels (SVM, Random Forest)
\end{enumerate}

This yields $2 \times 11 \times 2 = 44$ unique configurations, each replicated across $R = 20$ groups, resulting in $44 \times 20 = 880$ training-evaluation units.

\subsubsection{Combined Experimental Scale}

The complete experimental framework encompasses $132 + 44 = 176$ distinct configurations across both tracks, with $5,280 + 880 = 6,160$ total training-evaluation units. This comprehensive design enables systematic comparison between text-space generation (LLMs) and feature-space interpolation (SMOTE) under controlled conditions.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Ratio Progression}

We systematically vary the synthetic data proportion $\theta$ from 0\% (pure real data baseline) to 100\% (pure synthetic data) in 10\% increments. This granular progression enables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Detection of non-linear effects and threshold phenomena
    \item Sensitivity analysis to quantify performance degradation rates
    \item Identification of optimal mixing ratios for operational deployment
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Classification Models}

We evaluate two architecturally distinct classifiers to ensure findings generalize across different learning paradigms: Support Vector Machine (SVM, margin-based) and Random Forest (RF, ensemble-based). SVM employs RBF kernel with regularization $C = 1.0$, while Random Forest uses 100 trees with maximum depth 10. Both classifiers use balanced class weights to handle the 10\% spam ratio. Complete hyperparameter specifications are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:config}, Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

These architectures are selected for their complementary characteristics: SVM provides strong theoretical foundations and effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces, while Random Forest offers robustness to overfitting and natural handling of non-linear patterns and feature interactions.

\subsubsection{Feature Representation}

All emails are represented using TF-IDF features with maximum 10,000 features, unigram-bigram range (1-2), English stop word filtering, and lowercase normalization. This representation captures both word-level semantics and local phrase patterns relevant to spam detection. Complete TF-IDF configuration is detailed in Appendix~\ref{appendix:config}, Table~\ref{tab:tfidf_config}.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

Following the multi-faceted evaluation framework established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, we employ seven metrics (complete mathematical definitions in Appendix~\ref{appendix:metrics}):

\textbf{Primary metric}: F1-Score (harmonic mean of precision and recall, balanced assessment under class imbalance)

\textbf{Supporting metrics}: Accuracy (overall correctness), Precision (false positive control), Recall (detection sensitivity), AUC-ROC (threshold-independent discrimination), Specificity (true negative rate), and MCC (balanced measure considering all confusion matrix elements)

The F1-score serves as our primary evaluation metric due to its balanced treatment of precision and recall, particularly appropriate given the 10\% spam class imbalance.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Framework}

Following the statistical methodology framework established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, we apply the following specific configurations:

\subsubsection{Descriptive Statistics and Confidence Intervals}

For each experimental configuration, we compute sample mean $\bar{m}$ and standard deviation $s_m$ across $R = 20$ groups using Equations~3.2 in Methodology. We report 95\% confidence intervals ($\alpha = 0.05$) using the t-distribution with 19 degrees of freedom (Equation~3.3).

\subsubsection{Hypothesis Testing with FDR Correction}

We conduct paired t-tests following the hypothesis testing framework (Equation~3.4 in Methodology), comparing each synthetic ratio against the 0\% baseline. Given 140 simultaneous tests per configuration (10 non-baseline ratios $\times$ 2 classifiers $\times$ 7 metrics), we apply Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$ to control false discovery rate~\cite{benjamini1995controlling}.

\subsubsection{Effect Size Analysis}

We compute Cohen's $d$ standardized effect sizes (Equation~3.5 in Methodology) for all comparisons, interpreting magnitudes following conventional thresholds: negligible ($|d| < 0.2$), small ($0.2 \leq |d| < 0.5$), medium ($0.5 \leq |d| < 0.8$), and large ($|d| \geq 0.8$).

\subsubsection{Sensitivity Regression Analysis}

We apply the linear regression framework (Equation~3.6 in Methodology) to model performance degradation patterns. Following conventional guidelines, we interpret $R^2 > 0.9$ as indicating strong linear relationships suitable for extrapolation, while $R^2 < 0.7$ suggests non-linearities requiring empirical validation for specific proportions.

\subsection{Experimental Scale and Reproducibility}

Our comprehensive experimental framework encompasses substantial computational investment to ensure rigorous evaluation:

\textbf{Data Generation}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LLM generation}: 12,000 API calls across both models (2 models $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 100 spam/group $\times$ 3 prompts)
    \item \textbf{SMOTE generation}: 880 feature-space interpolation operations (2 strategies $\times$ 11 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 2 classifiers)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Model Training and Evaluation}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LLM track}: 5,280 classifier training-evaluation runs (2 models $\times$ 3 prompts $\times$ 2 strategies $\times$ 11 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 2 classifiers)
    \item \textbf{SMOTE track}: 880 classifier training-evaluation runs (2 strategies $\times$ 11 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 2 classifiers)
    \item \textbf{Total}: 6,160 training-evaluation units
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Statistical Analysis}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Approximately 15,000 hypothesis tests across all configurations, metrics, and comparisons
    \item FDR correction applied to control false discovery rate across multiple testing
\end{itemize}

All experiments employ fixed random seeds ($\text{seed} = 42$) throughout the pipeline—from data sampling and LLM generation to SMOTE interpolation and classifier training—ensuring complete reproducibility of all results. The systematic replication with $R = 20$ independent groups provides statistical power exceeding 80\% for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's $d \geq 0.5$) while maintaining computational feasibility. Detailed computational requirements and reproducibility measures are documented in Appendix~\ref{appendix:reproducibility}.

\subsection{Quality Control and Validation}

To ensure data quality and experimental validity, we implement multiple quality control mechanisms:

\subsubsection{LLM Generation Quality Checks}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Length constraints: $0.3 \leq \frac{|\text{synthetic}|}{|\text{original}|} \leq 3.0$
    \item Similarity bounds: $0.1 \leq \text{similarity}(\text{synthetic}, \text{original}) \leq 0.95$
    \item JSON parsing validation: All generated outputs must parse successfully
    \item Language quality: Automated checks for grammatical coherence
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{SMOTE Generation Quality Checks}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vector validity}: Verify all synthetic vectors contain finite values (no NaN or infinity)
    \item \textbf{Feature space bounds}: Ensure synthetic vectors remain within the convex hull of real data feature distributions
    \item \textbf{Sample count verification}: Confirm exact synthetic sample counts match target ratios for each configuration
    \item \textbf{Numerical stability}: Validate TF-IDF vectorization produces consistent feature dimensions across all experimental units
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Statistical Validation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributional checks}: Verify that group sampling maintains overall spam distribution
    \item \textbf{Assumption testing}: Validate normality and homoscedasticity assumptions for parametric tests
    \item \textbf{Robustness verification}: Supplement t-tests with non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests
    \item \textbf{Outlier analysis}: Identify and investigate extreme performance deviations
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reproducibility and Open Science}

To facilitate reproducibility and enable future research, we provide:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete configuration files specifying all experimental parameters
    \item Detailed random seed documentation for all stochastic processes
    \item Comprehensive logging of all experimental runs with timestamps
    \item Statistical analysis scripts with documented data processing steps
    \item Result tables with mean $\pm$ standard deviation and 95\% confidence intervals
\end{itemize}

All experimental code, configurations, and analysis scripts are version-controlled and documented to enable exact replication of our findings.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

Our experiments use publicly available spam datasets collected with appropriate consent and ethical review. The synthetic spam generation is conducted solely for research purposes to improve spam detection capabilities, with all generated data secured and not disseminated publicly to prevent misuse. We do not conduct experiments involving real user data or operational email systems.
