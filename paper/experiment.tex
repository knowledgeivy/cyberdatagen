\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiment}

This section describes our comprehensive experimental framework designed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness and limitations of LLM-generated synthetic spam data for training spam classifiers. Our design employs a multi-factorial approach with systematic replication to ensure statistical reliability and generalizability of findings.

\subsection{Dataset and Sampling Strategy}

\subsubsection{Source Dataset}

We conduct our experiments using the CEAS-08 spam email dataset~\cite{champa2024curated}, which contains 39,154 real-world email samples with 21,842 labeled as spam (55.8\%) and 17,312 as legitimate (44.2\%). This dataset provides a substantial corpus of authentic spam emails that capture diverse spam patterns and tactics observed in real-world email systems.

\subsubsection{Stratified Group Sampling}

Following the replicated design framework established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, we employ stratified random sampling to partition the spam corpus into $R = 20$ independent groups. This sample size is determined through power analysis (Equation~3.1 in Methodology): with $R = 20$, we achieve statistical power exceeding 80\% for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's $d \geq 0.5$) at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$, assuming typical performance variance $\sigma \approx 0.03$ observed in preliminary experiments.

Each group contains $N = 1,000$ samples with a fixed spam ratio of 10\% (100 spam, 900 legitimate), reflecting realistic class imbalance commonly observed in operational email filtering systems. The stratification process ensures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Statistical independence}: Each group is sampled without replacement, creating 20 statistically independent experimental units.
    \item \textbf{Distributional balance}: Stratified sampling maintains representation of diverse spam patterns across all groups.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Train-Test Split}

We employ a fixed out-of-sample test set to ensure consistent evaluation across all experimental conditions. The test set, comprising 20\% of the total dataset, is held out before any experimental manipulation and remains constant across all configurations. This approach eliminates test set variance as a confounding factor and enables fair comparison across different synthetic data strategies and ratios.

The training data from each group is further used to construct experimental datasets with varying synthetic data proportions, while the test set remains pure real-world data throughout all experiments.

\subsection{Synthetic Data Generation}

\subsubsection{LLM Configuration}

We employ two state-of-the-art large language models to evaluate robustness across different model architectures: \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini} (OpenAI) and \textbf{Claude-3.5-Haiku} (Anthropic). Both models use identical generation parameters (temperature $= 0.7$, max tokens $= 1{,}000$) ensuring fair comparison. Complete configuration details are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:config}, Table~\ref{tab:llm_config}.

\subsubsection{Prompt Engineering Strategies}

We design three prompt strategies varying in guidance intensity: \textbf{Original} (faithful reproduction of spam characteristics), \textbf{Strong} (amplified malicious features), and \textbf{Weak} (subtle spam signals). All prompts use consistent template structure with JSON-formatted output for parsing reliability. Complete templates are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:prompts}.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Data Mixing Strategies}

We implement two mixing strategies to evaluate the impact of distribution matching on synthetic data effectiveness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Within-Group}: Synthetic samples generated from the same group ($\text{Training}_i = \text{Real}_i^{(1-\theta)} \cup \text{Synthetic}_{i}^{(\theta)}$) tests in-distribution effectiveness
    \item \textbf{Cross-Group}: Synthetic samples from different groups ($\text{Training}_i = \text{Real}_i^{(1-\theta)} \cup \text{Synthetic}_{j \neq i}^{(\theta)}$) tests cross-distribution generalization
\end{itemize}

where $\theta$ denotes the synthetic proportion.

\textbf{Strategy Selection}: Preliminary experiments comparing both strategies across 3,300 measurements (2 models $\times$ 3 prompts $\times$ 11 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 2 classifiers $\times$ 2 strategies) revealed that cross-group mixing consistently outperforms within-group (mean F1: 0.563 vs. 0.554, $p < 10^{-6}$), though with small effect size (Cohen's $d = 0.13$). Given cross-group's marginal but consistent advantage and better alignment with real-world deployment scenarios where precise distribution matching is often infeasible, \textbf{all reported experiments employ cross-group mixing exclusively}.

\subsection{Experimental Factors and Design}

Our comprehensive experimental design encompasses two complementary evaluation tracks: \textbf{forward detection} evaluating whether LLM-generated synthetic spam data can effectively train spam classifiers, and \textbf{reverse detection} assessing whether classifiers trained on real data can detect AI-powered spam from different LLM sources.

\subsubsection{Forward Detection Track}

The forward detection experiments evaluate whether LLM-generated synthetic spam can effectively train classifiers to detect real spam. This track follows a full-factorial design with four primary factors:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model}: 2 levels (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku)
    \item \textbf{Prompt Strategy}: 3 levels (Original, Strong, Weak)
    \item \textbf{Mixing Strategy}: 1 level (Cross-Group only)
    \item \textbf{Synthetic Ratio} ($\theta$): 11 levels (0\%, 10\%, 20\%, ..., 100\%)
\end{enumerate}

This yields $2 \times 3 \times 1 \times 11 = 66$ unique experimental configurations. Each configuration is replicated across $R = 20$ independent groups and evaluated with 1 classifier (Support Vector Machine with optimized linear kernel, selected based on systematic hyperparameter tuning as described in Section~\ref{sec:experiment:hyperparameters}), resulting in $66 \times 20 \times 1 = 1,320$ training-evaluation units.

\textbf{Training-Testing Paradigm}: Each experiment trains on a mixture of real ham (900 samples) and varying proportions of real spam ($(1-\theta) \times 100$) combined with synthetic spam ($\theta \times 100$), then tests on a fixed out-of-sample set comprising real ham (900 samples) and real spam (100\% real). This setup evaluates whether synthetic data can substitute for real spam in training effective real-world spam detectors.

\subsubsection{Reverse Detection Track}

The reverse detection experiments address the complementary question: can classifiers trained on real spam data detect AI-generated spam from different LLM sources? This evaluates defensive capabilities against emerging AI-powered spam threats. We employ a \textbf{1-vs-Rest cross-model design}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPT-4.1-mini Testing}: Classifiers trained on real ham + Claude-3.5-Haiku synthetic spam, evaluated on detecting GPT-4.1-mini synthetic spam
    \item \textbf{Claude-3.5-Haiku Testing}: Classifiers trained on real ham + GPT-4.1-mini synthetic spam, evaluated on detecting Claude-3.5-Haiku synthetic spam
\end{itemize}

This design follows a factorial structure with five factors:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Testing Model}: 2 levels (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku)
    \item \textbf{Prompt Strategy}: 3 levels (Original, Strong, Weak)
    \item \textbf{Mixing Strategy}: 1 level (Cross-Group only)
    \item \textbf{Training Synthetic Ratio} ($\theta$): 3 levels (0\%, 50\%, 100\%)
    \item \textbf{Classifier}: 2 levels (SVM with linear kernel, Random Forest with optimized hyperparameters)
\end{enumerate}

This yields $2 \times 3 \times 1 \times 3 \times 2 = 36$ unique cross-model configurations, each replicated across $R = 20$ groups, resulting in $36 \times 20 = 720$ training-evaluation units. The reduced set of synthetic ratios (0\%, 50\%, 100\%) focuses on three critical scenarios: pure real baseline (0\%), balanced mixing (50\%), and pure synthetic (100\%).

\textbf{Training-Testing Paradigm}: For each experiment, the training set consists of real ham (900 samples) and real spam at the specified ratio, augmented with synthetic spam from the \emph{opposite} model. Critically, the test set comprises real ham (900 samples) and 100\% synthetic spam from the \emph{target} model being tested. This asymmetric design evaluates whether cross-model generalization occurs—whether features learned from one LLM's synthetic patterns transfer to detecting another LLM's synthetic spam. Unlike forward detection which uses SVM only, reverse detection employs both SVM and Random Forest to assess architectural differences in cross-model detection capabilities.

\subsubsection{Combined Experimental Scale}

The complete experimental framework comprises $66 + 36 = 102$ unique configurations with $1,320 + 720 = 2,040$ total training-evaluation units across both tracks. This substantial scale—systematically replicated across 20 independent groups—enables rigorous statistical analysis with high power ($>$80\%) for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's $d \geq 0.5$) while providing comprehensive coverage of LLM-based spam generation and detection scenarios.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Ratio Progression}

We systematically vary the synthetic data proportion $\theta$ from 0\% (pure real data baseline) to 100\% (pure synthetic data) in 10\% increments. This granular progression enables:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Detection of non-linear effects and threshold phenomena
    \item Sensitivity analysis to quantify performance degradation rates
    \item Identification of optimal mixing ratios for operational deployment
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Classification Models}

We employ two architecturally distinct classifiers across our experimental tracks to ensure findings generalize across different learning paradigms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Support Vector Machine (SVM)}: Margin-based classifier used in both forward and reverse detection tracks. Selected for its strong theoretical foundations and effectiveness in high-dimensional sparse feature spaces through margin maximization.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)}: Ensemble-based classifier used exclusively in reverse detection track. Included to assess architectural differences in cross-model detection capabilities, offering robustness to overfitting and natural handling of non-linear patterns through ensemble averaging.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Track-Specific Usage}: Forward detection experiments employ SVM only to maintain focus on LLM generation quality assessment, while reverse detection experiments employ both SVM and Random Forest to evaluate architectural robustness in detecting AI-powered spam from different sources.\label{sec:experiment:hyperparameters}

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Selection and Validation}

To ensure fair comparison across all experimental conditions and eliminate hyperparameter tuning as a confounding factor, we employ fixed hyperparameters validated through systematic grid search with 5-fold stratified cross-validation on a representative training set (Group 0, baseline configuration).

\textbf{Grid Search Design}: We evaluate SVM with C $\in \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$, kernel $\in \{\text{linear}, \text{rbf}\}$, gamma $\in \{\text{scale}, \text{auto}\}$, and Random Forest with n\_estimators $\in \{50, 100, 200\}$, max\_depth $\in \{5, 10, 20, \text{None}\}$, min\_samples\_split $\in \{2, 5\}$, min\_samples\_leaf $\in \{1, 2\}$, using F1-score as the primary optimization metric. Complete grid search results are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:hyperparameter_grid_search}.

\textbf{Key Findings}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest exhibits remarkable hyperparameter robustness}: Top configurations vary by less than 3\% F1-score (0.886--0.917), confirming the ensemble method's stability across diverse parameter settings.

    \item \textbf{SVM performance is invariant to regularization strength with linear kernel}: All C values $\in \{0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$ yield identical performance (F1 = 0.825 $\pm$ 0.031), indicating the TF-IDF feature space is well-separated and does not require regularization tuning.

    \item \textbf{Linear kernel substantially outperforms RBF kernel for SVM}: Linear kernel achieves F1 = 0.825, while RBF kernel achieves only F1 = 0.48--0.61, confirming that linear decision boundaries are more appropriate for high-dimensional sparse TF-IDF features (10,000 dimensions)~\cite{joachims1998text}.

    \item \textbf{Random Forest consistently outperforms SVM}: The best Random Forest configuration (F1 = 0.917) surpasses the best SVM configuration (F1 = 0.825) by 9.2 percentage points, demonstrating an architectural advantage independent of hyperparameter tuning.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Selected Hyperparameters} (employed throughout all experiments):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SVM}: C = 1.0, kernel = linear, class\_weight = balanced, random\_state = 42
    \item \textbf{Random Forest}: n\_estimators = 100, max\_depth = 10, min\_samples\_split = 5, min\_samples\_leaf = 2, class\_weight = balanced, random\_state = 42
\end{itemize}

These configurations represent stable, well-performing choices validated through cross-validation. The minimal hyperparameter sensitivity—especially for Random Forest (3\% performance range) and SVM linear kernel (0\% variation across all C values)—confirms that our findings regarding synthetic data effects, cross-model generalization, and architectural differences are robust to hyperparameter variations and reflect true phenomena rather than suboptimal tuning choices.

\subsubsection{Feature Representation}

All emails are represented using TF-IDF features with maximum 10,000 features, unigram-bigram range (1-2), English stop word filtering, and lowercase normalization. This representation captures both word-level semantics and local phrase patterns relevant to spam detection. Complete TF-IDF configuration is detailed in Appendix~\ref{appendix:config}, Table~\ref{tab:tfidf_config}.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

Following the multi-faceted evaluation framework established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, we employ seven metrics (complete mathematical definitions in Appendix~\ref{appendix:metrics}):

\textbf{Primary metric}: F1-Score (harmonic mean of precision and recall, balanced assessment under class imbalance)

\textbf{Supporting metrics}: Accuracy (overall correctness), Precision (false positive control), Recall (detection sensitivity), AUC-ROC (threshold-independent discrimination), Specificity (true negative rate), and MCC (balanced measure considering all confusion matrix elements)

The F1-score serves as our primary evaluation metric due to its balanced treatment of precision and recall, particularly appropriate given the 10\% spam class imbalance.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Framework}

We apply the comprehensive statistical methodology established in Section~\ref{sec:methodology} with the following specific configurations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Descriptive Statistics}: Mean and standard deviation across $R = 20$ groups with 95\% confidence intervals ($\alpha = 0.05$, 19 degrees of freedom)

    \item \textbf{Hypothesis Testing}: Paired t-tests comparing each synthetic ratio against the 0\% baseline, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at $\alpha = 0.05$ controlling false discovery rate across 140 simultaneous tests per configuration~\cite{benjamini1995controlling}

    \item \textbf{Effect Sizes}: Cohen's $d$ with conventional thresholds: negligible ($|d| < 0.2$), small ($0.2 \leq |d| < 0.5$), medium ($0.5 \leq |d| < 0.8$), large ($|d| \geq 0.8$)

    \item \textbf{Sensitivity Analysis}: Linear regression modeling performance vs synthetic proportion, with $R^2 > 0.9$ indicating strong linearity suitable for extrapolation, $R^2 < 0.7$ suggesting non-linearities requiring empirical validation
\end{itemize}

Complete mathematical formulations are provided in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

\subsection{Experimental Scale and Reproducibility}

Our comprehensive experimental framework encompasses substantial computational investment to ensure rigorous evaluation:

\textbf{Data Generation}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LLM generation}: 12,000 API calls across both models (2 models $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 100 spam/group $\times$ 3 prompts)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Model Training and Evaluation}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward detection track}: 1,320 SVM training-evaluation runs (2 models $\times$ 3 prompts $\times$ 1 strategy $\times$ 11 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 1 classifier)
    \item \textbf{Reverse detection track}: 720 training-evaluation runs (2 testing models $\times$ 3 prompts $\times$ 1 strategy $\times$ 3 ratios $\times$ 20 groups $\times$ 2 classifiers)
    \item \textbf{Total}: 2,040 training-evaluation units across both tracks
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Statistical Analysis}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Approximately 10,000 hypothesis tests across all configurations, metrics, and comparisons
    \item Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction applied to control false discovery rate across multiple testing
\end{itemize}

All experiments employ fixed random seeds ($\text{seed} = 42$) throughout the pipeline—from data sampling and LLM generation to classifier training—ensuring complete reproducibility of all results. The systematic replication with $R = 20$ independent groups provides statistical power exceeding 80\% for detecting medium effect sizes (Cohen's $d \geq 0.5$) while maintaining computational feasibility. Detailed computational requirements and reproducibility measures are documented in Appendix~\ref{appendix:reproducibility}.

\subsection{Quality Control and Validation}

We implement comprehensive quality control mechanisms to ensure data quality and experimental validity. For LLM-generated samples, we validate length constraints, similarity bounds, JSON parsing, and language quality. Statistical validation includes distributional checks, assumption testing (normality and homoscedasticity), robustness verification with non-parametric tests, and outlier analysis. Complete quality control specifications are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:quality_control}.

To facilitate reproducibility and enable future research, we provide:

    \item Detailed random seed documentation for all stochastic processes
    \item Comprehensive logging of all experimental runs with timestamps
    \item Statistical analysis scripts with documented data processing steps

All experimental code, configurations, and analysis scripts are version-controlled and documented to enable exact replication of our findings.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}

Our experiments use publicly available spam datasets collected with appropriate consent and ethical review. The synthetic spam generation is conducted solely for research purposes to improve spam detection capabilities, with all generated data secured and not disseminated publicly to prevent misuse. We do not conduct experiments involving real user data or operational email systems.
