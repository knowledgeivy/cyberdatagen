\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our comprehensive evaluation through 1,320 replicated experiments establishes fundamental principles governing LLM-generated synthetic spam data effectiveness while revealing critical dependencies on classifier architecture, mixing strategies, and generation approaches. The rigorous statistical framework with $R = 20$ independent replications enables confident assertions about synthetic data utility that extend beyond point estimates to quantify uncertainty, effect magnitudes, and degradation patterns. These findings provide both theoretical insights into synthetic data mechanisms and practical guidance for operational deployment in spam detection systems.

\subsection{Classifier Architecture as the Dominant Factor}

The most striking finding from our evaluation is the profound architectural dependence of synthetic data sensitivity. Random Forest demonstrates 11.8-fold greater robustness to synthetic data compared to SVM, experiencing only 2.1\% relative performance degradation at 100\% synthetic proportion versus SVM's 24.1\% degradation. This remarkable difference cannot be attributed to baseline performance disparities alone, as Random Forest maintains its advantage consistently across all synthetic ratios with minimal effect sizes (Cohen's $d = 0.19$-$0.47$) compared to SVM's substantial effects ($d = 0.35$-$2.02$).

\subsubsection{Theoretical Explanations for Architectural Sensitivity}

The differential sensitivity patterns suggest fundamentally different failure modes between margin-based and ensemble-based classifiers when confronted with distribution shift. SVM's reliance on support vectors for decision boundary construction makes it inherently vulnerable to distributional artifacts introduced by synthetic generation. If LLM-generated spam exhibits systematic deviations from real spam distributions—even subtle shifts in word co-occurrence patterns or semantic density—these perturbations directly impact support vector selection and consequently the entire decision boundary geometry.

The near-perfect linearity of SVM degradation ($R^2 = 0.990$) provides additional evidence for this distributional shift hypothesis. Linear degradation patterns indicate that each percentage point increase in synthetic proportion introduces consistent distributional contamination, accumulating monotonically without threshold effects or saturation phenomena. This pattern suggests that synthetic data does not fundamentally alter the classification problem structure but rather progressively shifts the feature distribution in ways that SVM's margin optimization cannot accommodate.

Random Forest's robustness likely stems from three complementary mechanisms. First, ensemble aggregation across 100 independently trained trees provides natural resistance to individual sample quality variations, as weak signals from lower-quality synthetic samples are averaged out by stronger signals from high-quality samples. Second, the bootstrap sampling mechanism during tree construction ensures that no single synthetic sample can disproportionately influence the ensemble, distributing risk across multiple subsamples. Third, the decision tree's hierarchical splitting strategy may naturally identify and route synthetic samples with unusual feature patterns down less influential branches, limiting their impact on overall predictions.

The moderate $R^2 = 0.723$ for Random Forest degradation supports this multi-mechanism hypothesis, as different resistance mechanisms may dominate at different synthetic proportions, producing non-linear patterns that reflect the complex interplay between bootstrap sampling, feature selection, and aggregation effects.

\subsubsection{Implications for Classifier Selection and Deployment}

These findings fundamentally alter classifier selection when synthetic data augmentation is anticipated. Organizations planning synthetic spam integration should prioritize ensemble methods over margin-based classifiers. SVM's predictable linear degradation ($\beta_1 = -0.00154$) enables performance forecasting but limits operational flexibility. Random Forest's minimal degradation even at 100\% synthetic enables aggressive utilization for privacy-sensitive applications or rapidly evolving threats where real samples are expensive to collect.

For operational deployment, these patterns translate to concrete guidance. SVM-based systems requiring synthetic augmentation should limit synthetic proportions below 30\% to maintain acceptable performance ($<$10\% relative degradation), leveraging the linear relationship for straightforward cost-benefit analysis. Random Forest systems, conversely, can operate effectively even with 100\% synthetic training data, offering deployment flexibility for privacy-constrained or legally restricted environments where real spam collection is impractical. The 11.8-fold robustness difference suggests that architectural choice may be the single most impactful decision for synthetic data utilization strategies.

\subsection{Understanding SMOTE's Distribution-Dependent Performance}

While Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} demonstrated SMOTE's aggregate Random Forest improvement (+3.7\%), the extreme group-to-group variance ($\pm$0.083, 4.2$\times$ higher than LLM methods) demands deeper theoretical investigation. This distribution-dependent behavior reveals fundamental differences between feature-space and text-space augmentation paradigms, with important implications for understanding when traditional oversampling methods provide value versus when LLM-based approaches offer superior reliability.

\subsubsection{Convex-Hull Interpolation Mechanism}

SMOTE's generation mechanism fundamentally differs from LLM text generation. Rather than probabilistically sampling from learned language distributions, SMOTE operates through deterministic feature-space interpolation. For each minority class sample $\mathbf{x}_i$, SMOTE identifies $k$ nearest neighbors (default $k=5$) in TF-IDF feature space and generates synthetic samples via linear interpolation:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{\text{synthetic}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda (\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i), \quad \lambda \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}_j$ is a randomly selected neighbor. This construction ensures all synthetic samples lie within the \emph{convex hull} of real minority class features, providing mathematically bounded generation constrained by observed feature distributions.

This constraint has profound implications. SMOTE cannot generate samples exhibiting feature combinations absent from real data—it fills gaps between existing samples but cannot extrapolate beyond their boundaries. When test samples fall within this convex hull, SMOTE's interpolated features provide enhanced coverage that improves classification boundaries. When test samples fall outside this hull, SMOTE provides no benefit and may even harm performance by inducing boundaries optimized for the wrong feature distribution.

\subsubsection{Why Random Forest Benefits While SVM Suffers}

The differential response between Random Forest (+3.7\%) and SVM (-19.3\%) reveals how classifier architectures interact with feature-space interpolation:

\textbf{Random Forest advantages}: Decision tree ensembles inherently benefit from continuous feature-space coverage. Each tree in the ensemble performs recursive binary splitting on feature values, and interpolated SMOTE samples provide continuous coverage along these splitting dimensions. The bootstrap sampling mechanism ensures diverse subsamples, amplifying the value of interpolated diversity. Feature bagging further enhances robustness: different trees examine different feature subspaces, and SMOTE's interpolation across all features provides coverage improvements visible to all trees. The ensemble averaging mechanism naturally mitigates individual poor-quality samples, as outliers in one tree are compensated by other trees trained on different bootstrap samples.

\textbf{SVM vulnerabilities}: Support Vector Machines construct decision boundaries based on support vectors—samples near the decision boundary. SMOTE's interpolation may generate synthetic samples that become spurious support vectors, pulling boundaries away from optimal positions. When test distributions differ from training, these SMOTE-induced boundary perturbations accumulate errors. The margin-based optimization amplifies small distributional shifts: if SMOTE shifts the feature distribution even slightly, the entire margin geometry adjusts to accommodate these synthetic patterns, potentially degrading generalization to real test samples.

The low $R^2 = 0.327$ for SMOTE's Random Forest regression confirms substantial non-linearity: improvements occur primarily at moderate synthetic ratios where interpolation fills genuine gaps, but plateau as additional interpolation provides diminishing returns. This contrasts with SVM's highly linear degradation ($R^2 = 0.969$), indicating consistent, predictable harm from distributional contamination.

\subsubsection{Distribution Alignment Hypothesis}

The bimodal outcome distribution—13 groups improving, 7 degrading—suggests performance depends critically on \emph{feature distribution alignment} between training and test sets. We hypothesize the following mechanism:

\textbf{High-improvement groups} (e.g., Group 10: +22.5\%): Low baseline performance (F1 = 0.719) indicates insufficient real spam coverage. Test spam features likely lie within the convex hull of training features, but real training samples provide only sparse coverage. SMOTE's interpolation fills critical gaps between sparse real samples, creating continuous feature coverage that significantly improves boundary robustness. Random Forest trees trained on this enhanced representation generalize better to test samples occupying previously sparse regions.

\textbf{High-degradation groups} (e.g., Group 0: -27.7\%): High baseline performance (F1 = 0.816) indicates strong real-data fit—training spam already provides excellent feature coverage for test spam. SMOTE's additional interpolation provides no benefit, as the feature space is already adequately covered. Moreover, synthetic interpolation may introduce artificial feature patterns that mislead boundary construction, particularly if test spam exhibits feature combinations not well-represented by convex interpolation of training features. The high baseline suggests real samples already capture test distribution well; synthetic augmentation disrupts this effective representation.

This hypothesis explains the negative correlation between baseline performance and SMOTE benefit: groups needing help most (low baselines due to insufficient coverage) benefit substantially, while groups already performing well (high baselines indicating good coverage) experience degradation when interpolation introduces unwanted perturbations.

\subsubsection{Comparison with LLM Stability}

LLM methods demonstrate fundamentally different variance profiles. GPT-4.1-mini and Claude-3.5-Haiku exhibit standard deviations 4-5$\times$ smaller than SMOTE, with consistent modest degradation across nearly all groups. This stability stems from text-space generation's inherent properties: LLMs sample from learned language distributions unconstrained by convex hull boundaries, enabling generation of diverse spam variants regardless of specific training sample configurations. While this flexibility introduces linguistic artifacts that degrade SVM performance (-24.1\% for GPT), it provides \emph{predictable} degradation independent of test distribution alignment.

The stability-performance trade-off crystallizes the fundamental paradigm difference:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SMOTE}: High potential improvement (+22.5\% maximum) but high risk (-27.7\% maximum), unpredictable a priori
    \item \textbf{LLMs}: Moderate, consistent degradation (-2.1\% typical), highly predictable behavior
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Practical Deployment Implications}

The distribution-dependent findings establish a risk-reward framework for method selection:

\textbf{Choose SMOTE when}: (1) Random Forest is the target classifier (SVM suffers consistent degradation), (2) pilot experiments confirm positive performance on validation sets, (3) test distributions likely align with training feature distributions, (4) maximum classification performance is paramount and text interpretability is unnecessary.

\textbf{Choose LLMs when}: (1) performance stability and predictability are critical, (2) test distribution alignment cannot be validated a priori, (3) text interpretability is required for human review or adversarial testing, (4) organizational risk tolerance favors consistent modest degradation over high-variance outcomes.

\textbf{Hybrid strategies}: Organizations might employ SMOTE for Random Forest training while using LLMs for interpretability-requiring tasks. Pilot testing with held-out validation groups can assess SMOTE suitability before production deployment. Adaptive methods that detect distribution alignment could switch between SMOTE and LLMs based on real-time alignment metrics.

\subsubsection{Limitations and Future Directions for Distribution Alignment Research}

Current work leaves critical questions unresolved. We lack \emph{predictive metrics} for identifying which groups will benefit from SMOTE before deployment—a critical gap for practical adoption. The \emph{convex hull hypothesis} requires direct testing through explicit computation of test sample positions relative to training convex hulls, correlating this geometric property with performance outcomes. Investigating \emph{alternative interpolation strategies} beyond linear SMOTE (e.g., Gaussian interpolation, learned manifold-based generation) might reduce variance while preserving benefits. Finally, \emph{hybrid text-feature approaches} that combine LLM semantic generation with SMOTE feature-space refinement represent unexplored territory potentially offering stability without sacrificing performance.

The extreme variance we observe positions SMOTE as a high-risk, high-reward strategy requiring careful validation, contrasting with LLMs' reliable but modest degradation. Understanding when feature-space versus text-space augmentation provides optimal outcomes remains a critical open question for advancing synthetic cybersecurity data effectiveness.

\subsection{The Surprising Irrelevance of Prompt Engineering}

The near-identical performance across our three systematically varied prompt strategies (original, strong, weak) stands as one of the most unexpected findings from our evaluation. With effect sizes below $d = 0.1$ and completely overlapping confidence intervals, the data provides strong evidence that prompt engineering intensity has negligible impact on downstream spam detection performance.

\subsubsection{Explanations for Prompt Strategy Convergence}

Several mechanisms may explain this finding. The semantic constraints of spam rewriting may leave limited room for prompt-driven variation, as spam emails balance competing objectives (persuasive content, promotional elements, urgency signals). GPT-4.1-mini's strong priors about spam characteristics may dominate over explicit instructions. The TF-IDF representation may lack sensitivity to dimensions where prompt strategies diverge, suggesting that deeper semantic representations might reveal effects not captured by lexical features. Finally, spam classifiers may learn coarse-grained patterns that generalize across multiple realizations of spam intent, providing natural resilience to generation intensity variations.

\subsubsection{Implications for Generation Pipeline Design}

The prompt strategy irrelevance finding fundamentally shifts optimization priorities for synthetic spam generation pipelines. Rather than investing in prompt optimization, sensitivity analysis, or A/B testing of generation instructions—efforts unlikely to yield meaningful performance improvements—practitioners should focus on LLM model selection (choosing more capable base models), seed sample quality (ensuring diverse, representative seeds), post-generation filtering (implementing distributional quality checks), and mixing ratio optimization (leveraging the clear performance-ratio relationships we observe). This reallocation of effort from prompt engineering toward more impactful pipeline components represents a significant shift in recommended best practices.

\subsection{Distribution Matching: Less Critical Than Anticipated}

The within-group versus cross-group mixing strategy comparison reveals that precise distribution matching between synthetic and real samples provides less benefit than intuition suggests. While within-group mixing should theoretically achieve superior distribution alignment by sourcing synthetic samples from the same spam groups as real training data, our results show minimal consistent performance differences between strategies, with effect sizes well below practical significance thresholds.

\subsubsection{Diversity-Fidelity Trade-offs}

The near-equivalence of mixing strategies suggests synthetic data effectiveness depends more on capturing general spam semantics than on precise statistical alignment. This supports a diversity-oriented view: synthetic samples provide value by expanding semantic space coverage rather than precisely replicating real sample statistics. The slight SVM advantage for cross-group at intermediate ratios hints that distributional diversity may compensate for quality issues, analogous to data augmentation in computer vision where controlled variation improves generalization.

\subsubsection{Operational Simplification}

From an operational perspective, the strategy equivalence finding substantially simplifies synthetic data deployment. Organizations need not maintain precise correspondence between synthetic and real data sources, enabling temporal flexibility (using historical synthetic data for current training), source diversity (pooling from multiple corpora without distribution matching concerns), organizational collaboration (sharing synthetic data without revealing specific spam distributions), and incremental adaptation (integrating new variants immediately without accumulation delays). This operational flexibility reduces deployment friction, potentially accelerating production adoption.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions of Large-Scale Replication}

Beyond the specific findings about synthetic spam data, our $R = 20$ replication design demonstrates the value of rigorous statistical methodology in synthetic data research. The comprehensive framework we establish—combining descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing with multiplicity correction, effect size quantification, and sensitivity analysis—provides a template for evaluating synthetic data approaches with confidence estimates and effect magnitude assessment.

\subsubsection{From Point Estimates to Distributional Understanding}

Single-trial evaluations provide point estimates that may be unrepresentative due to sampling variability. Our replicated design transforms these into distributional characterizations with quantified uncertainty. The 95\% confidence intervals reveal that Random Forest's small performance differences may reflect random variation rather than genuine effects, while SVM's non-overlapping intervals across most ratios provide strong evidence for genuine degradation. This signal-noise distinction would be impossible from single-trial results.

\subsubsection{Effect Sizes: Statistical vs Practical Significance}

Cohen's $d$ calculation distinguishes statistically significant effects from practically significant ones. SVM shows both statistical ($p < 0.001$) and practical significance ($d > 2.0$ at high ratios), indicating substantial degradation. Random Forest shows statistical significance but small effect sizes ($d < 0.5$), suggesting differences too small to influence deployment decisions. This distinction guides resource allocation: focus mitigation on combinations showing both significances (SVM), not just statistical significance (Random Forest).

\subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis for Prediction}

The regression framework enables performance forecasting for arbitrary synthetic proportions. SVM's high $R^2 = 0.990$ supports linear extrapolation: 35\% synthetic augmentation predicts $35 \times 0.00154 = 0.054$ F1-score degradation, enabling cost-benefit analysis. Random Forest's non-linear pattern ($R^2 = 0.723$) requires empirical validation rather than extrapolation, itself providing valuable information about prediction complexity.

\subsection{Limitations and Boundary Conditions}

Several limitations define generalizability boundaries. TF-IDF features capture lexical patterns but may miss semantic characteristics where deep learning models on contextualized embeddings might exhibit different sensitivity profiles. The CEAS-08 dataset represents spam from a specific time period; evolving adversarial tactics may interact differently with synthetic data. Our fixed 10\% spam ratio represents one point on the imbalance spectrum; more severe ($<$5\%) or balanced ($>$20\%) conditions may exhibit different patterns. GPT-4.1-mini represents current LLM capabilities; more advanced models might generate higher-quality synthetic data while weaker models might produce more obvious quality issues.
