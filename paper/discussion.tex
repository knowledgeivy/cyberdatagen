\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Model selection and distributional fidelity.} Cross-Track Synthesis reveals GPT-4.1-mini's superior generation quality stems from distributional alignment to real spam—preserving lexical patterns and statistical regularities enabling effective classifier training. However, this proximity makes GPT synthetic spam more detectable. Claude exhibits greater distributional divergence, reducing both training utility and detectability. This paradox suggests high-quality synthetic generation requires balancing fidelity for training against divergence for evasion—a tension GPT navigates through linguistic realism rather than distributional mimicry.

\textbf{Architectural robustness.} Random Forest's consistent superiority across all tracks stems from bootstrap aggregation's resistance to distributional shifts. Multiple decision trees trained on random feature/sample subsets capture diverse patterns while avoiding overfitting to synthetic artifacts. Voting mechanisms average out sensitivities to distributional anomalies, producing stable predictions. SVM's margin-based learning optimizes single hyperplanes vulnerable to systematic biases in synthetic distributions, yielding linear degradation (Track 1) and non-monotonic behavior (Track 2b). Organizations should deploy Random Forest for stability despite higher computational costs, or SVM for predictable degradation patterns enabling capacity planning.

\textbf{Cross-model augmentation and quality-diversity trade-offs.} Cross-Track Synthesis shows weak prompt training data provides larger detection improvements than strong prompts, revealing diversity drives cross-model generalization more than quality. This inverts optimization strategies: attackers should prioritize high-fidelity data (Track 1), while defenders should prioritize diverse, lower-quality data capturing broader feature spaces (Track 2b). Track 1's precision-recall asymmetry reveals synthetic training maintains high precision but degrades recall, creating overly conservative classifiers. Organizations must account for this bias, potentially requiring hybrid real-synthetic training to preserve recall.

\textbf{Asymmetric offense-defense dynamics.} LLMs favor attackers: low cost, minimal barriers, and high effectiveness (GPT maintains 86.6\% training utility) establish accessible offensive tools. Attackers leverage optimal models and prompts for either augmentation or evasion. Defenders face constraints: augmentation requires cross-model data, ensemble architectures, and precision-recall trade-offs. Model quality gaps create advantages defenders cannot fully mitigate. Defenders' primary resilience lies in Random Forest's inherent cross-model robustness. Overall, Generative AI shifts offense-defense balance toward attackers unless defenders adopt ensemble methods and cross-model augmentation proactively.

\textbf{Limitations.} TF-IDF captures lexical patterns but may miss semantics where deep learning with embeddings (BERT, RoBERTa) exhibits different sensitivities. CEAS-08 represents 2008 spam; modern corpora may interact differently with LLM-generated synthetic data. Fixed 10\% spam ratio represents one imbalance point; severe ($<$5\%) or balanced ($>$20\%) scenarios may differ. Our mid-tier models (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku) may not generalize to: (1) open-source LLMs (Llama 3, Mistral, Qwen) offering lower costs and local deployment; (2) budget (GPT-3.5-turbo) or frontier models (GPT-4o, o1) with different quality-cost trade-offs; (3) specialized approaches (template-based, fine-tuned models, hybrid methods). Track 2 focuses on cross-model detection; same-model scenarios remain unexplored. Spam detection may not generalize to other cybersecurity domains.
