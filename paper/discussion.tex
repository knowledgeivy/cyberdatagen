\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our experiments reveal three fundamental patterns across all evaluation tracks. First, model-dependent performance asymmetry persists throughout: GPT synthetic data maintains superior training effectiveness while exhibiting higher detectability, whereas Claude shows reduced utility and detectability. Second, ensemble architectures demonstrate consistent superiority over margin-based methods. Third, cross-model synthetic augmentation provides measurable detection improvements, with magnitudes varying by baseline performance, prompt strategy, and classifier architecture.

GPT's superior generation quality stems from alignment to real spam patterns, preserving lexical diversity and statistical regularities that enable effective classifier training. However, this proximity creates a fundamental paradox: the same fidelity that makes GPT synthetic spam valuable for training also renders it more detectable when classifiers are trained on real spam. Claude exhibits greater divergence, simultaneously reducing both training utility and detectability. This suggests high-quality synthetic generation inherently requires balancing fidelity for augmentation against divergence for evasion.

Random Forest's consistent superiority stems from bootstrap aggregation's resistance to distributional shifts. Multiple decision trees trained on random feature and sample subsets capture diverse patterns while avoiding overfitting to synthetic artifacts. Voting mechanisms average out sensitivities to anomalies, producing stable predictions across varying synthetic proportions. In contrast, SVM's margin-based learning optimizes single hyperplanes vulnerable to systematic biases, yielding linear degradation under same-model augmentation and non-monotonic behavior under cross-model training. Organizations should deploy Random Forest for stability, or SVM when predictable degradation patterns enable capacity planning.

Cross-model augmentation reveals that weak prompt training data provides larger detection improvements than strong prompts, demonstrating that diversity drives generalization more effectively than quality. This inverts optimization strategies: defenders should prioritize diverse, lower-quality data capturing broader feature spaces rather than training exclusively on high-quality samples.

LLMs fundamentally favor attackers through low cost, minimal technical barriers, and high effectiveness. Attackers can freely select optimal models and prompts for their objectives. Defenders face asymmetric constraints: effective augmentation requires cross-model data, ensemble architectures, and careful management of precision-recall trade-offs. Model quality gaps create advantages defenders cannot fully mitigate through architectural choices alone. Generative AI shifts the balance toward attackers unless defenders proactively adopt ensemble methods and cross-model augmentation strategies.

Our evaluation acknowledges several limitations. TF-IDF captures lexical patterns but may miss semantic relationships that deep learning approaches could reveal. The CEAS-08 corpus represents 2008 spam characteristics; modern corpora may interact differently with LLM-generated content. Our fixed class imbalance represents one operational point; different imbalance ratios may exhibit different patterns. Our mid-tier models may not generalize to open-source LLMs, frontier models, or specialized approaches including fine-tuned models. Our cross-model detection focus leaves same-model scenarios unexplored. Spam detection findings may not generalize to other cybersecurity domains requiring different feature representations or threat models.
