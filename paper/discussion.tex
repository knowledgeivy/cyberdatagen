\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our systematic evaluation across 4,080 training-evaluation units spanning two LLM models, three prompt strategies, multiple mixing ratios, and two classifier architectures reveals fundamental patterns governing LLM-generated synthetic spam effectiveness. The rigorous experimental design with 20 independent replications enables confident assertions extending beyond point estimates to quantify uncertainty, effect magnitudes, and performance boundaries. These findings illuminate both theoretical mechanisms underlying synthetic data utility and practical guidance for operational deployment, while revealing the asymmetric advantages Generative AI provides to attackers versus defenders in spam detection.

The most consequential finding concerns model selection for synthetic generation. GPT-4.1-mini demonstrates substantially superior generation quality compared to Claude-3.5-Haiku across all experimental tracks. In Synthetic-to-Real Detection, GPT maintains 73-87\% of baseline F1 performance at 100\% synthetic training, while Claude retains only 46-54\%—a 2-4$\times$ difference in degradation severity. This gap persists in Real-to-Synthetic Detection, where classifiers detect Claude synthetic spam significantly more easily (SVM F1: 0.335-0.589) than GPT synthetic spam (SVM F1: 0.636-0.744). The paradoxical relationship between training utility and detectability proves instructive: GPT's superior training effectiveness stems from closer distributional alignment to real spam, yet this same proximity makes GPT synthetic spam more detectable. This suggests that high-quality synthetic data generation requires achieving distributional fidelity sufficient for effective classifier training while maintaining enough divergence to evade detection—a tension that GPT navigates more successfully than Claude through subtle linguistic realism rather than distributional mimicry.

Prompt engineering emerges as a significant factor contrary to initial expectations. While both models exhibit substantial prompt sensitivity (GPT: 14\% F1 variance, Claude: 15\% variance at 100\% synthetic), the pattern reveals critical insights about generation quality control. Weaker prompts consistently produce lower-fidelity synthetic data that degrades both training utility (Track 1) and evasion capability (Track 2a). The inverse relationship between prompt strength and detectability in Real-to-Synthetic Detection demonstrates that weak prompts generate distributionally divergent spam easily distinguished from real samples, while original prompts produce the most deceptive synthetic spam. This finding contradicts simplified assumptions about prompt irrelevance and establishes prompt design as a critical quality control mechanism. Organizations deploying synthetic generation must carefully calibrate prompt strength to balance generation diversity against distributional fidelity, with original prompts providing optimal trade-offs in our evaluation.

Classifier architecture fundamentally determines synthetic data robustness. Random Forest demonstrates remarkable stability across all experimental conditions, maintaining consistent performance regardless of prompt strategy (F1 variance $<$5\% for GPT detection) while exhibiting minimal degradation even at extreme synthetic ratios. In contrast, SVM shows 40-76\% baseline variance across prompts and substantial sensitivity to synthetic proportion, with linear degradation patterns enabling predictable performance forecasting but limiting operational flexibility. The architectural difference extends to cross-model scenarios: Random Forest achieves superior improvements from cross-model augmentation (GPT detection: +8.5-10.2\% at 100\% synthetic) compared to SVM's non-monotonic behavior and occasional degradation. These patterns likely reflect ensemble methods' inherent resistance to distributional shifts through bootstrap aggregation and feature randomization, while margin-based classifiers remain vulnerable to systematic artifacts in synthetic feature distributions. For operational deployment, this translates to concrete guidance: organizations prioritizing stability and cross-model robustness should deploy Random Forest architectures, while those requiring predictable degradation patterns for capacity planning may prefer SVM despite higher sensitivity.

The precision-recall asymmetry observed in Synthetic-to-Real Detection reveals a fundamental limitation of synthetic training data. Classifiers trained on 100\% synthetic spam maintain high precision (0.95-1.00) while suffering severe recall degradation (0.76 $\rightarrow$ 0.30-0.60), indicating they become overly conservative—correctly identifying spam when detected but missing many actual spam instances. This pattern suggests synthetic spam captures surface-level lexical features enabling spam recognition but fails to represent the full distributional diversity of real spam, causing classifiers to reject borderline cases. The asymmetry carries important operational implications: synthetic-trained systems may achieve acceptable false positive rates but unacceptable false negative rates, potentially allowing substantial spam to evade detection. Organizations must account for this bias when establishing acceptance criteria, potentially requiring higher recall thresholds or hybrid training strategies mixing real and synthetic data to preserve recall performance.

Cross-model synthetic augmentation demonstrates the potential for "fighting fire with fire" defensive strategies, though benefits prove target-dependent and architecturally contingent. Training with synthetic spam from one LLM improves detection of another LLM's spam by 29-46\% (Claude detection) or 8-10\% (GPT detection) with Random Forest at 100\% synthetic augmentation. However, the counterintuitive finding that weak prompt training data provides larger improvements than strong prompt data (+17\% relative) suggests that diversity rather than quality drives cross-model generalization. Lower-quality, more divergent synthetic spam may better capture the full feature space of AI-generated content, enabling robust detection across varied generation strategies. This pattern indicates that defenders seeking maximum cross-model robustness should prioritize diverse synthetic training data over high-fidelity generation, fundamentally inverting the quality-diversity trade-off compared to training augmentation scenarios.

These findings illuminate the fundamental asymmetry in Generative AI's impact on spam detection. For attackers, LLMs provide substantial advantages: GPT-4.1-mini generates synthetic spam that maintains 73\% training effectiveness when substituted for real spam, while original prompts produce spam difficult to detect even with augmented training data (Real-to-Synthetic baseline F1: 0.744 for GPT). The low cost (\$1.80 for 6,000 emails), minimal technical barriers, and demonstrated evasion capabilities establish LLMs as accessible, effective offensive tools. For defenders, benefits prove more constrained: synthetic augmentation improves detection but requires cross-model training data, architectural choices favoring robustness over performance, and acceptance of precision-recall trade-offs. The 2-4$\times$ degradation gap between GPT and Claude generation quality suggests attackers selecting optimal models gain advantages defenders cannot fully mitigate through augmentation alone. Defenders' primary advantage lies in Random Forest's inherent cross-model robustness (F1 $\approx$ 0.80-0.89 across all conditions), providing baseline resilience independent of augmentation. Overall, Generative AI appears to favor attackers in spam generation while providing defenders with limited but measurable countermeasures, shifting the traditional offense-defense balance toward attackers unless defenders proactively adopt ensemble architectures and cross-model augmentation strategies.

Several limitations constrain generalizability. Our TF-IDF feature representation captures lexical patterns but may miss semantic characteristics where deep learning models with contextualized embeddings might exhibit different sensitivity profiles. The CEAS-08 dataset represents 2008 spam characteristics; evolving adversarial tactics and linguistic patterns may interact differently with modern LLM-generated synthetic data. Our fixed 10\% spam ratio represents one point on the class imbalance spectrum; more severe imbalance ($<$5\%) or near-balanced scenarios ($>$20\%) may produce different degradation patterns. The two LLM models we evaluate (GPT-4.1-mini, Claude-3.5-Haiku) represent mid-tier capabilities optimized for cost-performance trade-offs; frontier models with superior language understanding or smaller models with quality limitations might alter the offense-defense balance. Track 2 experiments focus on cross-model detection scenarios, while same-model detection (attackers and defenders using identical LLMs) remains unexplored and may exhibit different patterns. Finally, our spam detection context may not generalize to other cybersecurity domains where synthetic data characteristics and detection requirements differ fundamentally from email classification tasks.
