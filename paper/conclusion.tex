\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presents a rigorous evaluation framework for LLM-generated synthetic spam data through systematic replication and comprehensive statistical analysis. Our investigation of 1,320 experimental configurations across 20 independent groups establishes fundamental principles governing synthetic data effectiveness while quantifying the critical dependencies on classifier architecture, synthetic proportion, and generation strategies that determine practical utility.

\subsection{Principal Findings}

Our systematic evaluation reveals four fundamental insights that reshape understanding of synthetic cybersecurity data utility, spanning both neural text generation and traditional feature-space augmentation paradigms.

\subsubsection{Classifier Architecture Dominates Synthetic Data Sensitivity}

The most consequential finding is the dramatic architectural dependence of synthetic data sensitivity. Random Forest demonstrates 11.8-fold greater robustness than SVM, maintaining 97.9\% of baseline performance at 100\% synthetic proportion compared to SVM's 75.9\% retention. This architectural difference manifests in fundamentally different degradation patterns: SVM exhibits highly linear performance decline ($R^2 = 0.990$) at -0.154\% F1-score per 1\% synthetic increase, while Random Forest shows non-linear degradation ($R^2 = 0.723$) with 11.8-fold smaller slope magnitude.

These patterns suggest that ensemble methods' inherent aggregation mechanisms and bootstrap sampling provide natural resistance to individual sample quality variations, while margin-based classifiers' reliance on support vectors makes them vulnerable to distributional artifacts introduced by synthetic generation. This architectural sensitivity has immediate implications: organizations planning synthetic data integration should prioritize ensemble classifiers, while SVM users must carefully limit synthetic proportions to maintain acceptable performance.

\subsubsection{Prompt Engineering Shows Minimal Impact}

Contrary to intuitive expectations, our three systematically varied prompt strategies (original, strong, weak) produce statistically indistinguishable results, with effect sizes below Cohen's $d = 0.1$ and completely overlapping confidence intervals. This finding suggests that fundamental LLM generation capabilities dominate over specific prompt engineering choices for spam generation tasks, likely due to semantic constraints inherent in maintaining malicious intent while rewriting content.

This unexpected result has practical value: optimization efforts should focus on LLM model selection, seed sample quality, and mixing ratio determination rather than extensive prompt tuning. The limited returns from prompt engineering enable simpler generation pipelines that bypass expensive prompt optimization phases.

\subsubsection{Distribution Matching Flexibility}

The near-equivalence of within-group and cross-group mixing strategies demonstrates that precise distribution matching between synthetic and real training samples provides less benefit than anticipated. This finding simplifies operational deployment by enabling temporal flexibility (historical synthetic data augmenting current training), source diversity (pooling synthetic samples from multiple corpora), and organizational collaboration (sharing synthetic data without revealing specific spam distributions).

The slight performance variations between strategies remain within practical tolerance thresholds, suggesting that synthetic data utility depends more on capturing general spam semantics than on precise statistical alignment with specific training distributions.

\subsubsection{Traditional Feature-Space vs Neural Text-Space Augmentation}

Our systematic comparison of SMOTE (feature-space interpolation) against LLM-based generation (text-space neural synthesis) reveals fundamentally different risk-reward profiles with critical implications for method selection. SMOTE demonstrates extreme performance variability across experimental groups (standard deviation $\pm$0.083, 4.2$\times$ higher than GPT-4.1-mini's $\pm$0.020), with 13 of 20 groups showing improvement but 7 experiencing degradation when applied to Random Forest. The aggregate +3.7\% Random Forest improvement masks dramatic group-to-group differences ranging from +22.5\% (Group 10) to -27.7\% (Group 0).

This distribution-dependent behavior stems from SMOTE's convex-hull interpolation mechanism: performance depends critically on whether test samples fall within the feature-space boundaries defined by training samples. When test distributions align with training—indicated by low baseline performance suggesting sparse coverage—SMOTE's interpolated samples fill critical gaps and substantially improve classification. When training already provides adequate coverage—indicated by high baseline performance—SMOTE's additional interpolation provides no benefit and may introduce unwanted perturbations.

In contrast, LLM methods exhibit 4-5$\times$ lower variance with consistent modest degradation (GPT: -2.1\%, Claude: -1.6\% for Random Forest) across nearly all groups, providing predictable behavior independent of specific distribution alignment. This stability-performance trade-off crystallizes a fundamental paradigm difference: SMOTE offers high potential improvement but unpredictable risk, while LLMs provide reliable but modest degradation with the critical advantage of generating interpretable text for human review, adversarial testing, and cross-system transfer.

The comparison establishes that method selection should be driven by operational requirements: SMOTE for maximum Random Forest performance when pilot validation confirms positive effects, LLMs when stability, predictability, and text interpretability are paramount. Notably, SMOTE universally degrades SVM performance (-19.3\% aggregate), eliminating it as a viable option for margin-based classifiers and reinforcing the dominance of classifier architecture in determining synthetic data utility.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}

Beyond specific findings about synthetic spam data, this work establishes rigorous evaluation standards for synthetic cybersecurity data research through several methodological innovations.

\subsubsection{Large-Scale Replication for Statistical Confidence}

The $R = 20$ replication design transforms synthetic data evaluation from exploratory point estimation to confirmatory hypothesis testing with quantified uncertainty. The systematic application of paired t-tests with FDR correction, Cohen's $d$ effect sizes, and 95\% confidence intervals enables principled distinction between statistically significant effects (reliably detectable) and practically significant effects (large enough to influence operational decisions).

This framework reveals that Random Forest, despite showing statistically significant degradation at many synthetic ratios, exhibits effect sizes ($d = 0.19$-$0.47$) below practical significance thresholds. Conversely, SVM shows both statistical and practical significance ($d = 0.35$-$2.02$), indicating genuine degradation requiring mitigation. Single-trial evaluations cannot make such distinctions, potentially leading to erroneous deployment decisions.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis for Performance Prediction}

The linear regression framework modeling performance as a function of synthetic ratio enables predictive planning for operational deployment. The regression slopes provide actionable metrics: SVM's -0.00154 slope indicates that each 1\% synthetic increase costs 0.154\% F1-score, enabling straightforward cost-benefit analysis for scenarios trading data acquisition costs against performance penalties.

The $R^2$ metric distinguishes between linear degradation patterns (enabling simple extrapolation) and non-linear patterns (requiring empirical validation for specific ratios). This distinction itself provides valuable deployment guidance about whether performance can be predicted from limited experiments or requires comprehensive evaluation.

\subsection{Practical Implications and Operational Guidance}

Our findings provide strategic guidance for organizations deploying synthetic spam data. The architectural dependence suggests that classifier selection represents the most impactful decision: ensemble methods enable aggressive synthetic utilization while margin-based classifiers require careful proportion management. Organizations should prioritize architectural choice over peripheral optimization efforts.

The SMOTE vs LLM comparison establishes a decision framework based on operational priorities. Organizations should choose SMOTE when: (1) Random Forest is the target classifier (as SVM universally degrades), (2) maximum classification performance is paramount and pilot validation confirms positive effects on held-out validation sets, (3) test distributions likely align with training feature distributions, and (4) text interpretability is unnecessary. Conversely, LLMs should be preferred when: (1) performance stability and predictability outweigh maximum performance potential, (2) test distribution alignment cannot be validated a priori, (3) text interpretability is required for human review or adversarial testing, and (4) organizational risk tolerance favors consistent modest degradation over high-variance outcomes. Hybrid strategies combining SMOTE for training with LLMs for interpretability-requiring tasks may offer optimal risk-reward balance.

The prompt engineering irrelevance finding redirects optimization resources toward fundamentally impactful factors—LLM model capability, seed sample diversity, and post-generation quality control—rather than intensive prompt tuning. This resource reallocation enables more efficient generation pipelines without sacrificing effectiveness.

The distribution matching flexibility simplifies deployment logistics, enabling organizations to leverage synthetic data from diverse sources, time periods, and collaborators without requiring precise statistical alignment. This operational flexibility reduces friction for adoption in privacy-constrained, legally restricted, or rapidly evolving threat environments where real spam collection faces practical barriers.


\subsection{Future Research Directions}

Our findings suggest several critical directions for advancing synthetic cybersecurity data research:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deep learning evaluation}: Extending the framework to transformer-based classifiers operating on contextualized embeddings to test whether learned representations increase robustness or sensitivity to synthetic artifacts.

    \item \textbf{Predictive distribution alignment metrics}: Developing metrics to predict a priori which groups will benefit from SMOTE before deployment, addressing the critical gap preventing confident SMOTE adoption. This could involve explicit convex hull computation, test sample position analysis relative to training boundaries, or learned predictors of distribution alignment quality.

    \item \textbf{Advanced interpolation strategies}: Investigating alternatives to linear SMOTE interpolation—including Gaussian interpolation, learned manifold-based generation, or adaptive k-neighbor selection—to reduce variance while preserving performance benefits.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid text-feature approaches}: Exploring methods combining LLM semantic generation with SMOTE feature-space refinement, potentially achieving stability without sacrificing performance by leveraging complementary strengths of both paradigms.

    \item \textbf{Adversarial robustness}: Systematic testing of whether synthetic-trained classifiers exhibit different adversarial vulnerability compared to real-trained classifiers, informing safe deployment practices.

    \item \textbf{Temporal adaptation}: Evaluating synthetic data's contribution to handling concept drift as spam patterns evolve, particularly for generating synthetic examples of emerging threats from limited initial samples.

    \item \textbf{Multimodal extension}: Applying the framework to network traffic, malware binaries, and fraud detection to test generalizability across cybersecurity data modalities beyond text, assessing whether feature-space vs text-space distinctions apply to non-textual domains.

    \item \textbf{Active learning integration}: Combining synthetic generation with active learning to optimize the balance between selective real sample labeling and synthetic gap-filling, potentially using SMOTE for exploration and LLMs for exploitation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Broader Impact}

The practical impact of this work extends to multiple stakeholder communities. For cybersecurity researchers, the validated framework provides rigorous evaluation protocols that enable methodologically sound synthetic data research and meaningful comparison across different generation approaches. For operational security teams, the quantitative performance-proportion relationships support evidence-based decisions about synthetic data deployment in resource-constrained environments.

The demonstrated effectiveness of synthetic data has immediate implications for privacy-preserving threat intelligence sharing, where organizations can generate and share synthetic samples without revealing sensitive real attack patterns or distribution characteristics. This capability addresses legal and competitive constraints that currently limit collaborative cybersecurity research.

The accessibility of synthetic generation compared to expensive real data collection may democratize cybersecurity research, enabling academic institutions and small organizations without access to large-scale real attack datasets to conduct meaningful work on realistic problems. Our rigorous evaluation framework ensures that such synthetic-based research meets credibility standards comparable to real-data research.
