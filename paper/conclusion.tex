\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work provides the first systematic evaluation of Generative AI's dual impact on spam detection, simultaneously assessing LLM-generated synthetic data's utility for classifier training and the challenges it poses for detecting AI-powered spam. Through 2,280 training-evaluation units spanning two LLM models, three prompt strategies, multiple mixing ratios, two classifier architectures, and 20 independent replications, we establish rigorous empirical foundations for understanding how Large Language Models reshape the offense-defense balance in spam detection.

Our Synthetic-to-Real Detection experiments reveal that LLM-generated synthetic spam can partially substitute for real training data, though with model-dependent degradation. GPT-4.1-mini maintains 73-87\% of baseline F1 performance when training on 100\% synthetic data, while Claude-3.5-Haiku retains only 46-54\%—a 2-4$\times$ quality gap that establishes model selection as the primary determinant of synthetic data utility. The observed precision-recall asymmetry, where synthetic-trained classifiers maintain high precision (0.95-1.00) but suffer severe recall degradation (0.30-0.60), reveals a fundamental limitation: synthetic spam captures surface-level lexical patterns enabling recognition but fails to represent the full distributional diversity of real spam, causing systems to miss many actual spam instances. Prompt engineering proves consequential contrary to initial expectations, with original prompts providing optimal generation quality and weak prompts producing 14-27\% lower performance—establishing prompt design as a critical quality control mechanism rather than a negligible factor.

Real-to-Synthetic Detection experiments establish that classifiers trained solely on real spam achieve moderate zero-shot detection of AI-generated spam (F1: 0.532-0.824), indicating substantial distributional overlap between real and synthetic spam. However, detection performance varies dramatically by target model (GPT more detectable than Claude), prompt strategy (weak prompts most detectable, original prompts most evasive), and training data quantity (SVM improves +17-18\% with 2$\times$ real data, Random Forest stable). Critically, the inverse relationship between prompt strength and detectability demonstrates that high-quality generation (original prompts) produces spam difficult to distinguish from real samples, while lower-quality generation (weak prompts) creates easily detectable distributional divergence. This pattern suggests attackers selecting optimal models and prompts can generate spam that evades detection even when defenders train on substantial real data.

Mixed-to-Synthetic Detection experiments demonstrate that cross-model synthetic augmentation provides measurable defensive benefits, though architecturally contingent. Training with synthetic spam from one LLM improves detection of another LLM's spam by 29-46\% (Claude detection) or 8-10\% (GPT detection) with Random Forest at 100\% synthetic augmentation, validating "fighting fire with fire" strategies. However, the counterintuitive finding that weak prompt training data provides larger improvements than strong prompt data (+17\% relative) reveals that diversity rather than quality drives cross-model generalization—lower-quality, more divergent synthetic spam better captures the full feature space of AI-generated content. Random Forest consistently outperforms SVM across all tracks, exhibiting minimal degradation ($<$5\% variance), superior cross-model robustness (F1 $\approx$ 0.80-0.89), and prompt invariance, while SVM shows 40-76\% baseline variance and substantial sensitivity to synthetic proportion.

These findings illuminate a fundamental asymmetry in Generative AI's impact on spam detection. For attackers, LLMs provide substantial advantages: accessible tools (\$1.80 for 6,000 emails), high-quality generation (GPT maintains 73\% training effectiveness), and demonstrated evasion capabilities (baseline detection F1: 0.744). The 2-4$\times$ quality gap between GPT and Claude establishes that attackers selecting optimal models gain advantages defenders cannot fully mitigate through augmentation alone. For defenders, benefits prove more constrained: synthetic augmentation improves cross-model detection but requires architectural choices favoring robustness (Random Forest), acceptance of precision-recall trade-offs, and diverse rather than high-quality training data. Defenders' primary advantage lies in Random Forest's inherent cross-model robustness independent of augmentation, providing baseline resilience that requires no additional data collection. Overall, Generative AI appears to favor attackers in spam generation while providing defenders with limited but measurable countermeasures, shifting the traditional offense-defense balance toward attackers unless defenders proactively adopt ensemble architectures and cross-model augmentation strategies.

The practical implications extend beyond spam detection to broader cybersecurity contexts. Our rigorous experimental framework—combining replicated trials, comprehensive statistical analysis, and systematic exploration of generation parameters—provides a template for evaluating synthetic data approaches in other domains with quantified uncertainty and effect magnitude assessment. The demonstrated model-dependent quality gaps establish that LLM selection represents the most impactful decision for both offensive generation and defensive augmentation, far exceeding returns from prompt engineering or mixing strategy optimization. Organizations deploying synthetic data should prioritize Random Forest architectures to minimize degradation risk, calibrate prompt strength to balance diversity against fidelity, and leverage cross-model augmentation for improved detection robustness.

Several critical directions warrant future investigation. Extending evaluation to transformer-based classifiers operating on contextualized embeddings may reveal whether learned semantic representations increase robustness or sensitivity to synthetic artifacts compared to TF-IDF features. Evaluating same-model detection scenarios (attackers and defenders using identical LLMs) would complete the threat landscape characterization beyond our cross-model focus. Testing frontier models with superior language understanding or smaller models with quality limitations would establish generalizability boundaries for our model-dependent findings. Investigating temporal robustness as spam patterns evolve would assess whether synthetic-trained systems maintain effectiveness against concept drift. Finally, extending the framework to network traffic, malware analysis, and fraud detection would test whether our findings generalize beyond email classification to other cybersecurity modalities.

The accessibility of LLM-based synthetic generation, demonstrated effectiveness for training augmentation, and validated cross-model detection improvements collectively establish Generative AI as a transformative technology for spam detection research and operations. However, the asymmetric advantages favoring attackers, substantial quality gaps between models, and architectural dependencies determining utility underscore that effective deployment requires careful model selection, architectural choices, and realistic expectations about both offensive threats and defensive capabilities. As LLM capabilities continue advancing, the offense-defense dynamics we characterize will likely evolve, necessitating ongoing evaluation to maintain empirically grounded understanding of Generative AI's role in cybersecurity.
