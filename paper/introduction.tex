\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Generative AI, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs), has fundamentally transformed the landscape of spam and phishing attacks. Traditional spam relied on template-based generation with characteristic indicators, spelling errors, grammar mistakes, repetitive patterns that enabled relatively straightforward detection. Modern LLMs eliminate these telltale signs, producing spam emails with natural language characteristics, contextual coherence, and grammatical correctness that closely mimic legitimate communications. This transformation presents attackers with unprecedented capabilities: automated generation of convincing phishing campaigns at scale, personalized spear-phishing attacks leveraging web-scraped information, and adaptive content generation that evades pattern-based detection systems. Simultaneously, LLMs offer defenders new capabilities for addressing persistent training data challenges. Spam detection systems require large, diverse training datasets, yet acquiring such data confronts privacy constraints, legal restrictions on maintaining malicious content, and the inherent rarity of spam samples in operational environments where class imbalance represents the norm. LLMs promise to alleviate these constraints through synthetic data generation, potentially augmenting limited real datasets without requiring access to sensitive information or expensive manual collection efforts.

This duality raises a fundamental question that motivates our research: Does Generative AI favor attackers or defenders in spam detection? Answering this question requires systematic evaluation of both offensive capabilities (whether LLM-generated spam evades detection) and defensive capabilities (whether synthetic training data improves detection systems).

\subsection{Research Questions}

Our investigation addresses three complementary research questions that systematically examine whether Generative AI favors attackers or defenders:

\textbf{RQ1: Can LLM-generated synthetic spam effectively substitute for real training data?}

Spam detection systems require large, diverse training datasets, yet acquiring such data confronts fundamental obstacles: privacy regulations restricting retention of personal communications, legal constraints on maintaining malicious content, computational costs of manual labeling, and the inherent rarity of spam in operational environments where legitimate email vastly outnumbers attacks. These barriers limit defensive capabilities particularly for resource-constrained organizations, new threat variants lacking labeled examples, and privacy-sensitive domains where data sharing remains prohibited. If LLM-generated synthetic spam can substitute for real training data without performance degradation, defenders gain a transformative capability to build detection systems without accessing sensitive data, rapidly respond to emerging threats, and democratize spam defense across organizations lacking access to large-scale real datasets.

\textbf{RQ2a: Can traditional spam detectors recognize LLM-generated attacks without prior exposure?}

As attackers gain access to increasingly capable language models, the operational question facing defenders is whether existing detection systems trained exclusively on historical spam remain effective against AI-generated attacks they have never encountered. This zero-shot detection scenario represents the current threat landscape: organizations deploy spam filters trained on years of real attack data, yet suddenly face adversaries wielding LLMs capable of generating grammatically flawless, contextually coherent phishing emails at scale. If traditional detectors fail to recognize synthetic spam, defenders face a critical capability gap requiring urgent mitigation strategies. Conversely, if real spam and synthetic spam share sufficient statistical patterns, existing defenses may provide inherent resilience against AI-powered attacks without requiring system updates.

\textbf{RQ2b: Does augmenting training data with synthetic spam from available LLMs improve detection of attacks from different LLM sources?}

Defenders typically cannot predict which specific LLM adversaries will employ for attack generation. This uncertainty raises a strategic question about cross-model generalization: can defenders leverage readily available LLMs (e.g., commercial APIs they control) to generate synthetic training data that improves detection of attacks from different LLM sources (e.g., models used by adversaries)? If cross-model augmentation proves effective, defenders gain a proactive capability to strengthen detection systems against unknown AI-powered threats by "fighting fire with fire"—using their own LLM access to prepare for adversary LLM attacks. However, if synthetic data only improves detection of spam from the same generation source, defenders must either acquire diverse LLM access or accept reduced effectiveness against varied adversary tooling.

\subsection{Principal Contributions}

This work makes several fundamental contributions to understanding Generative AI's role in spam detection:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Dual-Track Evaluation.} We provide the first systematic evaluation of LLMs' dual impact on spam detection, simultaneously assessing whether synthetic data can substitute for real training examples and whether traditional defenses recognize AI-generated attacks. This comprehensive approach reveals asymmetric advantages: attackers gain accessible, high-quality generation capabilities while defenders obtain limited but measurable countermeasures through architectural choices and cross-model augmentation.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Statistical Framework.} Through replicated experimental design with 20 independent groups, we establish evaluation standards for synthetic cybersecurity data research that enable hypothesis testing with quantified uncertainty and effect magnitudes rather than exploratory point estimation. Linear degradation models relating synthetic proportion to performance enable practitioners to predict detection effectiveness for arbitrary mixing ratios and establish performance boundaries for operational deployment.

    \item \textbf{Cross-Model Generalization with Quantitative Performance Models.} Our cross-LLM evaluation design demonstrates that training with synthetic spam from one model improves detection of attacks from different models by 29-46\%, validating "fighting fire with fire" strategies. We establish quantitative degradation patterns showing 2-4$\times$ quality gaps between generation sources, revealing that model selection represents the most impactful decision for both offensive generation and defensive augmentation—far exceeding returns from prompt engineering or mixing strategy optimization.

    \item \textbf{Architectural Robustness Analysis.} We demonstrate that classifier architecture fundamentally determines synthetic data utility: ensemble methods maintain stable performance regardless of synthetic proportion (Random Forest: $<$5\% degradation at 100\% synthetic for high-quality generation), while margin-based classifiers exhibit substantial sensitivity (SVM: 13-46\% degradation). This finding establishes architectural selection as a critical defensive capability, with ensemble methods providing inherent resilience against distributional shifts between real and synthetic spam without requiring augmentation.

    \item \textbf{Evidence-Based Resolution of Offense-Defense Balance.} We provide empirically-grounded answers to whether Generative AI favors attackers or defenders, revealing fundamental asymmetries: attackers obtain accessible tools generating effective spam at low cost, while defenders gain measurable but architecturally-contingent countermeasures requiring ensemble methods and cross-model augmentation. The precision-recall trade-offs we identify (synthetic-trained systems maintain high precision but suffer severe recall degradation) translate directly to operational guidance for both offensive evaluation and defensive deployment strategies.

    \item \textbf{Public Benchmark Dataset.} We release a comprehensive benchmark comprising 32,000 email samples enabling reproducibility and standardized evaluation for future research on LLM-generated spam detection.\footnote{Dataset and complete experimental configurations available at: \url{https://github.com/tisage/llm-synthetic-spam-benchmark}}

\end{enumerate}
