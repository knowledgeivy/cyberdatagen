\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}



% \subsection{Spam and Phishing Detection}

Spam and phishing email detection has evolved through multiple paradigm shifts driven by adversarial co-evolution between attackers and defenders. Early systems relied on rule-based filtering and manually curated blacklists~\cite{al2023multi,magdy2022efficient}, which proved brittle against adaptive attackers who could trivially modify content to evade static pattern matching. The field subsequently transitioned to machine learning approaches, with statistical classifiers including Naive Bayes~\cite{kontsewaya2021evaluating,wijaya2023spam}, Support Vector Machines~\cite{budiman2024email,wijaya2023spam}, and ensemble methods~\cite{wijaya2023spam,bazzaz2021hybrid,kontsewaya2021evaluating,wang2025hybrid} demonstrating superior generalization across diverse datasets through learned feature representations. Recent advances in deep learning have further improved detection capabilities, with transformer-based models leveraging contextualized embeddings to capture semantic patterns beyond surface-level lexical features~\cite{altwaijry2024advancing}. Systematic reviews~\cite{thakur2023systematic} document consistent progress across architectures, datasets, and evaluation metrics, establishing spam detection as a mature application domain for supervised learning.

However, two persistent challenges limit practical deployment effectiveness. First, severe class imbalance characterizes operational environments where malicious emails constitute only 1-10\% of total traffic, with some domains experiencing even more extreme ratios~\cite{bazzaz2021hybrid,bazzaz2021hybrid,johari2025key}. This imbalance causes classifiers to develop strong bias toward majority classes, sacrificing minority class sensitivity for overall accuracy~\cite{bazzaz2021hybrid,johari2025key,wang2025hybrid}. Traditional mitigation strategies including cost-sensitive learning and threshold adjustment provide marginal improvement but cannot overcome the fundamental limitation of insufficient minority class training examples~\cite{gao2025comprehensivesurveyimbalanceddata,wang2025hybrid}. Second, privacy regulations and legal constraints restrict retention and sharing of email data, limiting access to large-scale training datasets particularly for resource-constrained organizations and emerging threat variants~\cite{li2024spamdam,kim2023pcsf}. These dual challenges—insufficient training examples and restricted data access—motivate exploration of synthetic data generation as a potential solution.

% \subsection{Synthetic Data Generation for Cybersecurity}

Synthetic data generation addresses training data scarcity by creating artificial examples that augment limited real datasets. Traditional approaches in natural language processing have explored augmentation techniques including synonym replacement, back-translation, and paraphrasing~\cite{shorten2021text}, with comparative studies~\cite{ghadekar2023text} assessing their effectiveness across text classification tasks. While these methods introduce lexical diversity, they lack semantic understanding and cannot generate content with controlled characteristics while maintaining realistic coherence.

Deep generative models offer more sophisticated generation capabilities through learned distributional representations. Generative Adversarial Networks learn complex data distributions via adversarial training, enabling high-fidelity synthetic sample generation for structured data~\cite{bourou2021review,ammara2025syntheticnetworktrafficdata,10765501}. Applications to cybersecurity have demonstrated effectiveness for network traffic generation~\cite{ammara2025syntheticnetworktrafficdata} and intrusion detection datasets~\cite{10765501,ammara2025syntheticnetworktrafficdata}, with systematic reviews~\cite{agrawal2024review} documenting consistent advantages over traditional augmentation methods for tabular data. However, GANs face critical limitations for text generation. GAN architectures operate primarily on numerical and categorical features, struggling to generate semantically coherent natural language that maintains logical consistency across multiple interdependent attributes~\cite{zhao2024ctab,li2024hda}. Training instability, substantial data requirements, and lack of fine-grained control over semantic characteristics further limit applicability to email spam generation where linguistic realism and controllable malicious intent representation prove essential.

Large Language Models have fundamentally transformed synthetic text generation capabilities through pre-training on massive corpora spanning diverse domains and linguistic patterns. Unlike feature-space interpolation or adversarial training, LLMs generate text through autoregressive prediction conditioned on context, enabling semantically coherent content that maintains consistency across multiple attributes~\cite{razmyslovich2025eltex,almorjan2025large}. Recent applications to cybersecurity have explored domain-driven synthetic text generation for classification tasks~\cite{razmyslovich2025eltex,zhang2025llms,ammara2024synthetic}, structured data creation incorporating domain knowledge~\cite{almorjan2025large}, and general cyber defense applications~\cite{hassanin2024comprehensive}. Systematic reviews analyzing hundreds of LLM applications~\cite{zhang2025llms,ding2023tmg} document broad adoption across security domains, while emerging research explores prompt engineering strategies for controlled generation~\cite{chen2025unleashing,de2023unsupervised}.

However, existing work exhibits three critical limitations motivating our research. First, prior evaluations focus exclusively on defensive utility. Whether synthetic data improves classifier training without assessing offensive threats that LLMs pose through evasive spam generation. This asymmetric framing ignores the dual-use nature of generative AI, where the same capabilities enabling defenders to augment training data also empower attackers to generate spam evading detection. Second, systematic evaluation of prompt engineering strategies specifically for cybersecurity remains limited, with existing guides providing generic recommendations rather than empirically grounded analysis of how prompt variations affect generation quality and detectability in adversarial contexts. Third, cross-model generalization whether training on synthetic data from one LLM improves detection of spam from different LLM sources remains unexplored despite critical operational importance for defenders who cannot predict adversary model selection.

% \subsection{Adversarial Applications of Generative AI}

While substantial research has explored defensive applications of LLMs for cybersecurity, emerging work has begun examining offensive capabilities and adversarial threats. Recent studies have demonstrated LLMs' potential for generating convincing phishing emails~\cite{bethany2024large,afane2024next,weinz2025impact}, automating social engineering attacks~\cite{ai2024defending,thapa2025large}, and evading content moderation systems~\cite{afane2024next,josten2024investigating}. These offensive capabilities raise fundamental questions about the offense-defense balance in cybersecurity: do generative AI advances favor attackers or defenders?

Prior work addressing this question has focused on specific attack scenarios or defensive techniques in isolation, lacking comprehensive evaluation frameworks that simultaneously assess both offensive threats and defensive countermeasures under controlled experimental conditions. Furthermore, existing evaluations typically rely on single-model assessments without exploring cross-model dynamics where attackers and defenders may employ different LLM sources. The accessibility and low cost of commercial LLM APIs have democratized sophisticated spam generation capabilities previously requiring substantial technical expertise, yet systematic assessment of whether this democratization shifts the traditional offense-defense balance remains absent from the literature.

% \subsection{Research Gaps and Contributions}

Our work addresses three critical gaps in existing research. First, we provide the first systematic dual-track evaluation simultaneously assessing LLMs' offensive utility and defensive utility, revealing whether Generative AI provides net advantages to attackers or defenders. Second, we establish rigorous experimental methodology employing replicated trials, comprehensive statistical analysis, and systematic exploration of generation parameters, enabling hypothesis testing with quantified uncertainty rather than exploratory point estimation common in prior work. Third, we investigate cross-model generalization through evaluation designs where training and testing employ different LLM sources, addressing practical scenarios where defenders cannot predict adversary model selection. These contributions advance understanding of Generative AI's role in spam detection beyond single-sided defensive evaluations toward comprehensive characterization of evolving offense-defense dynamics.
