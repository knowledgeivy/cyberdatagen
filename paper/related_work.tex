\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Spam and phishing email detection represents a longstanding challenge in cybersecurity, with adversarial threats continuously evolving to evade detection systems. Early approaches relied on rule-based filtering and manually curated blacklists, which proved fragile against adaptive attackers. The field subsequently transitioned toward machine learning-based detection, with statistical classifiers including Naive Bayes, Support Vector Machines, and ensemble methods demonstrating effectiveness across diverse datasets [In-Depth Analysis of Phishing Email Detection, 2025]. Recent advances in deep learning, particularly transformer-based models, have further improved detection capabilities [Advancing Phishing Email Detection: A Comparative Study of Deep Learning Models, 2024], with systematic reviews [A Systematic Review on Deep-Learning-Based Phishing Email Detection, 2023] documenting consistent progress across multiple approaches and datasets.

Despite these algorithmic advances, severe class imbalance remains a fundamental limitation for practical deployment. In operational settings, malicious emails typically constitute only 1-10\% of total traffic, with some environments experiencing even more extreme ratios. This imbalance causes machine learning models to develop strong bias toward majority classes, resulting in poor sensitivity to critical minority class detection~\cite{5356528}. Lian et al.~\cite{lian2021robustness} demonstrated the importance of systematic evaluation under varying imbalance conditions, showing that model performance degrades unpredictably as imbalance severity increases. Recent work [Addressing Class Imbalance in Intrusion Detection: A Comprehensive Evaluation of Machine Learning Approaches, 2024] has explored machine learning approaches under extreme imbalance ratios, while specialized research [Extreme minority class detection in imbalanced data for network intrusion, 2023] has addressed scenarios where minority classes constitute less than 1\% of the data. Traditional solutions including cost-sensitive learning and threshold adjustment provide some improvement but cannot fundamentally overcome the limitation of insufficient minority class training examples. This scarcity of representative training data motivates the exploration of synthetic data generation.

The challenge of class imbalance has driven substantial research into synthetic data generation methods. Traditional approaches emerged from statistical and data mining communities, with the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling TEchnique (SMOTE)~\cite{chawla2002smote} establishing the foundational paradigm. SMOTE generates new instances through linear interpolation between k-nearest neighbors in feature space, effectively expanding minority class decision regions. Numerous variants have refined this approach, with systematic studies [Resampling imbalanced data for network intrusion detection datasets, 2021] evaluating these techniques across cybersecurity datasets and recent work [Machine learning-based network intrusion detection for big and imbalanced data, 2024] exploring combinations with feature embedding. However, SMOTE and its variants share fundamental constraints. These methods operate purely in feature space, generating synthetic feature vectors that cannot be mapped back to interpretable content. When applied to text data, SMOTE must first transform emails into numerical representations, perform interpolation, and use synthetic vectors directly for training, preventing generation of actual email content. Parallel developments in natural language processing have explored text-specific augmentation techniques including Easy Data Augmentation (EDA) and back-translation [Text Data Augmentation for Deep Learning, 2021], with comparative studies [Text Data Augmentation Using Generative Adversarial Networks, Back Translation and EDA, 2023] assessing their effectiveness. While these techniques provide some diversity, they lack semantic understanding and cannot generate content with specific characteristics while maintaining realistic coherence across multiple attributes.

The emergence of deep generative models marked a significant paradigm shift. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) learn complex data distributions through adversarial training, enabling generation of high-fidelity synthetic samples. Ammara et al.~\cite{ammara2025syntheticnetworktrafficdata} demonstrated GAN-based methods including CTGAN and CopulaGAN outperforming traditional approaches in capturing complex distributions, while Zouhri and Idri~\cite{10765501} applied CTGAN to address multi-class imbalance in cybersecurity datasets. Recent advances [CTAB-GAN+: enhancing tabular data synthesis, Zhao et al., 2023] have achieved substantial improvements on intrusion detection datasets, with applications extending to IoT security [Imbalanced tabular data modelization using CTGAN and machine learning to improve IoT Botnet attacks detection, 2023] and resource-constrained environments [Intrusion Detection Using CTGAN and Lightweight Neural Network for Internet of Things, Das et al., 2025]. Systematic reviews [A Review of Generative Models in Generating Synthetic Attack Data for Cybersecurity, 2024] and comparative analyses [Synthetic Data Generation in Cybersecurity: A Comparative Analysis, 2024] have documented consistent superiority of GAN-based methods over traditional approaches. However, GANs face critical limitations for our research problem. GAN-based methods including CTGAN primarily operate on numerical and categorical features, making them unsuitable for generating natural language content found in emails. While GANs excel at structured tabular data, they struggle to generate semantically coherent text maintaining logical consistency across multiple attributes. Additionally, GAN training suffers from stability issues and requires substantial training data, limiting applicability in low-resource scenarios. Most critically, existing GAN approaches cannot support fine-grained control over specific semantic characteristics of generated content, such as subtlety or overtness of malicious features.

The recent emergence of Large Language Models has fundamentally transformed synthetic data generation capabilities for textual content. Pre-trained on massive corpora, LLMs demonstrate remarkable ability to generate human-readable text across diverse domains. Razmyslovich et al.~\cite{razmyslovich2025eltex} introduced domain-driven synthetic text generation using LLMs for cybersecurity classification tasks. Recent systematic reviews [When LLMs meet cybersecurity: a systematic literature review, 2025] have analyzed hundreds of works across diverse LLM applications, while comprehensive overviews [A Comprehensive Overview of Large Language Models (LLMs) for Cyber Defences: Opportunities and Directions, 2024] have explored their potential in cyber defence. Practical applications have extended to structured data creation, with recent work [Large Language Models for Synthetic Dataset Generation of Cybersecurity Indicators of Compromise, 2025] applying fine-tuned LLMs to generate synthetic datasets incorporating domain knowledge. LLMs offer several critical advantages over traditional and GAN-based methods. First, LLMs generate semantically coherent content maintaining logical consistency across multiple attributes, which cannot be achieved through feature-space interpolation or tabular data generation. Second, LLMs support fine-grained control through prompt engineering, enabling generation of content with specific characteristics. Third, LLMs require minimal training data, making them applicable in low-resource scenarios. However, LLM-based generation introduces new challenges. Generated content quality is highly sensitive to prompt design, yet systematic evaluation of prompt engineering strategies specifically for cybersecurity data generation remains limited. Recent work [Prompt Engineering for Structured Data: A Comparative Evaluation of Styles and LLM Performance, 2025] has begun exploring prompt variations across leading LLMs, with comprehensive guides [Unleashing the potential of prompt engineering for large language models, 2025] synthesizing emerging practices, but these remain generic rather than tailored to cybersecurity requirements.

Despite substantial progress across traditional, GAN-based, and LLM-based synthetic data generation, several critical gaps limit practical deployment. First, no systematic comparison evaluates these fundamentally different generation paradigms under identical experimental conditions. While individual studies demonstrate benefits of specific approaches, the absence of controlled comparisons makes it impossible to provide evidence-based recommendations regarding which method best addresses particular operational requirements. Second, existing work evaluates synthetic generation primarily under balanced or mildly imbalanced conditions, while real-world cybersecurity applications face extreme imbalance. The effectiveness of different generation methods under realistic conditions remains unexplored. Third, systematic investigation of prompt engineering for LLM-based generation is lacking. While prompt design clearly affects generated content, existing work focuses on task-specific optimization rather than establishing general principles. The impact of different prompt strategies on quality, distributional properties, and downstream performance remains poorly understood, particularly regarding how prompts control subtle semantic characteristics relevant to security applications.

Our work addresses these gaps through several key contributions. We provide the first systematic comparison of traditional (SMOTE), GAN-based (CTGAN), and LLM-based synthetic generation methods for spam detection under identical experimental conditions. We conduct systematic evaluation under realistic class imbalance that characterizes operational deployments. We perform controlled experiments with different prompt strategies, establishing evidence-based guidelines for LLM-based synthetic data generation in cybersecurity applications and demonstrating how prompt engineering systematically controls semantic properties relevant to security detection tasks.
