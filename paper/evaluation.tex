\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness and limitations of LLM-generated synthetic spam data through systematic analysis across 1,320 experimental configurations (66 unique conditions replicated across 20 independent groups). The results reveal complex performance patterns that depend critically on classifier architecture, synthetic data proportion, mixing strategy, and prompt engineering approach. Through rigorous statistical analysis incorporating confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and sensitivity metrics, we establish quantitative relationships between synthetic data characteristics and practical utility for spam detection.

\subsection{Overall Performance Analysis}

The classification experiments reveal fundamental differences in how Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest classifiers respond to synthetic data integration. Table~\ref{tab:performance_summary} presents baseline performance (0\% synthetic) and degradation patterns across synthetic proportions for the original prompt strategy with within-group mixing.

\begin{table}[h]

\label{tab:performance_summary}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Classifier} & \textbf{Baseline} & \textbf{100\% Synthetic} & \textbf{Degradation} \\
\textbf{} & \textbf{(Mean $\pm$ SD)} & \textbf{(Mean $\pm$ SD)} & \textbf{(Relative)} \\
\midrule
SVM & 0.627 $\pm$ 0.026 & 0.476 $\pm$ 0.038 & -24.1\% \\
Random Forest & 0.793 $\pm$ 0.041 & 0.777 $\pm$ 0.034 & -2.1\% \\

\end{table}

Random Forest demonstrates remarkable robustness to synthetic data integration, maintaining 97.9\% of baseline performance even when trained entirely on synthetic samples. This resilience aligns with ensemble methods' inherent ability to aggregate diverse patterns and mitigate individual sample quality issues. In contrast, SVM exhibits substantial sensitivity to synthetic data quality, experiencing 24.1\% relative performance degradation at 100\% synthetic proportion. This pattern suggests that margin-based classifiers may be more vulnerable to distributional shifts introduced by LLM generation artifacts.

The substantially higher baseline performance of Random Forest (F1 = 0.793 vs. 0.627 for SVM) indicates superior fit to the spam detection task characteristics. This performance gap persists across all synthetic proportions, with Random Forest maintaining consistent advantages of 0.15-0.30 F1 points. The 95\% confidence intervals, ranging from $\pm$0.026 to $\pm$0.043 across conditions, demonstrate reasonable experimental stability while quantifying estimation uncertainty inherent in our 20-group replication design.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis: Performance Degradation Patterns}

To quantify the impact of synthetic data proportion on classifier performance, we conduct linear regression analysis modeling F1-score as a function of synthetic ratio ($\theta$). Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity_analysis} presents sensitivity metrics for both classifiers under original prompt, within-group mixing conditions.

\begin{table}[h]

\label{tab:sensitivity_analysis}

& \textbf{($\beta_1$)} & & & \textbf{(0$\rightarrow$100\%)} \\
\midrule

The regression analysis reveals striking differences in degradation patterns between classifier architectures. SVM demonstrates highly linear performance degradation ($R^2 = 0.990$), declining by 0.154\% F1-score per 1\% increase in synthetic proportion. This near-perfect linear relationship ($p < 10^{-9}$) suggests that SVM performance degrades consistently and predictably as synthetic data replaces real samples, with no apparent threshold effects or non-linearities across the 0-100\% range.

Random Forest exhibits more complex degradation patterns, with moderate $R^2 = 0.723$ indicating substantial non-linearity in the performance-proportion relationship. While still statistically significant ($p < 0.001$), the weaker linear fit suggests threshold effects, saturation phenomena, or varying sensitivity across different synthetic proportion ranges. The 11.8-fold smaller slope magnitude (-0.00013 vs. -0.00154) quantifies Random Forest's superior robustness, experiencing only 1.6\% total degradation compared to SVM's 15.1\% degradation over the full synthetic proportion range.

The visualizations confirm the divergent sensitivity patterns while demonstrating that confidence interval overlap between baseline and low synthetic proportions ($\leq$20\%) is minimal for SVM but substantial for Random Forest across both mixing strategies.

Contrary to intuitive expectations, cross-group mixing (where synthetic samples originate from different spam groups than the real training samples) does not consistently underperform within-group mixing. For SVM at moderate synthetic proportions (30-50\%), cross-group strategy actually achieves superior performance, with F1-scores 0.013 to 0.017 higher than within-group at these ratios. This advantage disappears at 100\% synthetic, where both strategies converge to nearly identical performance (0.476 vs. 0.476).

Random Forest exhibits opposite but more subtle patterns, showing slight advantages for within-group mixing at moderate proportions but surprisingly better cross-group performance at 100\% synthetic (0.788 vs. 0.777, $\Delta$ = 0.011). These non-monotonic and classifier-dependent patterns suggest complex interactions between distribution matching, diversity, and generalization properties that warrant deeper investigation.

The overlapping 95\% confidence intervals for most strategy comparisons indicate that observed differences may not reach statistical significance, suggesting that mixing strategy selection may be less critical than other factors such as prompt engineering approach or overall synthetic proportion. However, the consistency of mid-range cross-group advantages for SVM hints at potential benefits from synthetic data diversity that partially compensates for distribution mismatch.

This finding suggests that the fundamental LLM generation process, rather than specific prompt engineering choices, dominates synthetic data quality for spam detection tasks.

The slight trend toward original and strong strategies outperforming weak strategies aligns with the hypothesis that more explicit spam characteristic preservation benefits classifier training. However, effect sizes (Cohen's $d < 0.1$) fall well below meaningful thresholds, indicating that prompt optimization yields marginal returns compared to other experimental factors.

This robustness to prompt variation contrasts with findings in other NLP domains where prompt engineering substantially impacts model behavior. For spam generation, the inherent semantic constraints of maintaining malicious intent while rewriting content may limit the effective variation space that different prompts can explore.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Hypothesis Testing}

To rigorously assess performance degradation significance, we conduct paired t-tests comparing each synthetic ratio against the baseline (0\% synthetic) for each classifier. With 10 comparisons per classifier across 7 performance metrics, we apply Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate correction ($\alpha = 0.05$) to control for multiple testing.

For SVM F1-score under original prompt, within-group conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{10\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.009, FDR-corrected $p = 2.30\times10^{-10}$, Cohen's $d = 0.35$ (small-medium effect)
    \item \textbf{30\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.032, FDR-corrected $p = 1.96\times10^{-26}$, Cohen's $d = 1.15$ (very large effect)
    \item \textbf{50\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.062, FDR-corrected $p = 3.77\times10^{-35}$, Cohen's $d = 2.02$ (very large effect)
\end{itemize}

All synthetic ratios from 10-100\% show highly significant degradation ($p < 0.001$ after FDR correction) with effect sizes increasing monotonically from small (10\%) to very large (50-100\%). The non-overlapping 95\% confidence intervals between baseline and all synthetic proportions $\geq$10\% provide visual confirmation of statistical significance, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:performance_degradation}.

For Random Forest F1-score, significance patterns differ markedly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{10\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.008, FDR-corrected $p = 2.05\times10^{-3}$, Cohen's $d = 0.19$ (small effect)
    \item \textbf{30\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.011, FDR-corrected $p = 6.09\times10^{-3}$, Cohen's $d = 0.27$ (small effect)
    \item \textbf{100\% synthetic}: Mean difference = 0.016, FDR-corrected $p = 1.26\times10^{-7}$, Cohen's $d = 0.47$ (medium effect)
\end{itemize}

While still statistically significant, Random Forest shows substantially smaller effect sizes across all proportions, with the maximum (100\% synthetic) only reaching medium effect magnitude. The frequent overlapping confidence intervals between adjacent synthetic ratios indicate that Random Forest performance degrades gradually without sharp threshold transitions.

\subsection{Practical Implications and Operational Guidelines}

The comprehensive evaluation results inform practical deployment strategies for synthetic spam data. For systems employing SVM classifiers, synthetic data integration requires careful proportion management, with our results suggesting that synthetic ratios should remain below 30\% to limit performance degradation to acceptable levels ($<$10\% relative loss). The highly linear degradation pattern enables straightforward performance prediction and cost-benefit analysis for specific operational scenarios.

Random Forest classifiers, demonstrating 20-fold greater robustness to synthetic data, offer more flexibility for synthetic data utilization. The minimal degradation even at 100\% synthetic (2.1\% relative loss) suggests viability for scenarios where real spam samples are expensive, privacy-sensitive, or legally constrained. However, the non-linear degradation pattern ($R^2 = 0.723$) necessitates empirical validation for specific proportion choices rather than relying on linear extrapolation.

The indistinguishability of within-group and cross-group mixing strategies at high synthetic proportions simplifies deployment logistics, as precise distribution matching between synthetic and real data sources appears unnecessary. This finding reduces operational complexity for scenarios requiring synthetic data from diverse sources or time periods.

The minimal impact of prompt engineering strategy suggests that generation pipeline optimization should prioritize other factors such as seed sample quality, LLM model selection, or post-generation filtering rather than extensive prompt tuning. This resource allocation guidance enables more efficient synthetic data generation workflows.

\subsection{Limitations and Boundary Conditions}

Several important limitations constrain the generalizability of our findings. First, our evaluation employs TF-IDF feature representation, which captures surface-level lexical patterns but may miss deeper semantic or structural anomalies in synthetic text. Deep learning classifiers operating directly on embeddings might exhibit different sensitivity profiles, potentially showing either enhanced robustness through learned representations or increased vulnerability to distributional artifacts.

Second, the fixed 10\% spam ratio in our experimental design reflects only one point in the operational class imbalance spectrum. Systems operating under severe imbalance ($<$5\% spam) or more balanced conditions ($>$20\% spam) may experience different degradation patterns, particularly if classifier decision boundaries shift substantially across imbalance levels.

Third, the CEAS-08 dataset, while substantial and realistic, represents spam patterns from a specific time period and threat landscape. Evolving spam tactics, especially those incorporating adversarial machine learning techniques, may exhibit different synthetic generation quality and classifier sensitivity characteristics.

Finally, our evaluation focuses exclusively on classification performance metrics, not addressing potential model calibration issues, out-of-distribution generalization to novel spam variants, or adversarial robustness properties that may differ systematically between real and synthetic training regimes.

\subsection{Key Findings Summary}

Our comprehensive evaluation establishes several critical findings regarding LLM-generated synthetic spam data effectiveness:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classifier-dependent sensitivity}: Random Forest demonstrates 11.8-fold greater robustness to synthetic data than SVM (2.1\% vs. 24.1\% degradation at 100\% synthetic), indicating that classifier architecture selection is paramount for synthetic data utilization strategies.

    \item \textbf{Linear degradation for SVM}: SVM performance decreases highly linearly ($R^2 = 0.990$) with synthetic proportion, enabling straightforward performance prediction and cost-benefit analysis. The -0.154\% F1 change per 1\% synthetic serves as a practical planning metric.

    \item \textbf{Non-linear patterns for Random Forest}: The moderate $R^2 = 0.723$ for Random Forest indicates threshold effects or saturation phenomena, necessitating empirical validation rather than linear extrapolation for specific deployment scenarios.

    \item \textbf{Strategy robustness}: Within-group and cross-group mixing strategies show minimal consistent performance differences, suggesting that precise distribution matching is less critical than anticipated. This simplifies operational deployment across diverse data sources.

    \item \textbf{Prompt engineering limits}: The three prompt strategies yield statistically indistinguishable results (effect sizes $d < 0.1$), indicating that fundamental LLM generation capabilities dominate over specific prompt engineering choices for this task.

    \item \textbf{Statistical rigor validation}: FDR-corrected hypothesis tests confirm significant degradation at all synthetic proportions $\geq$10\% for SVM, while Random Forest shows significance but with substantially smaller effect sizes ($d = 0.19$-$0.47$ vs. $d = 0.35$-$2.02$).
\end{enumerate}

These findings establish synthetic spam data as a viable but classifier-dependent approach for augmenting training datasets, with clear quantitative guidance for operational deployment decisions. The systematic framework we present provides empirical benchmarks for future research in synthetic cybersecurity data generation and establishes rigorous standards for evaluation methodology in this rapidly evolving domain.
