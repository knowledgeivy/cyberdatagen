\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Our evaluation systematically assesses LLM-generated synthetic spam across three complementary experimental tracks spanning 2,280 training-evaluation units (2 models × 3 prompts × multiple configurations × 20 replications). Following the methodology framework (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), we present results organized by research questions: Track 1 evaluates synthetic data for classifier training (RQ1), Track 2a establishes baseline detection capabilities against AI-powered spam (RQ2a), and Track 2b investigates hybrid training strategies (RQ2b). All experiments employ cross-group mixing strategy based on preliminary analysis showing consistent advantages over within-group mixing (detailed comparison in Appendix~\ref{appendix:mixing_strategy_comparison}).

\subsection{Track 1: Synthetic-to-Real Detection}
\label{sec:eval:track1}

This track addresses RQ1: \textit{Can LLM-generated synthetic spam effectively train classifiers to detect real spam?} We systematically vary synthetic proportion $\theta$ from 0\% (pure real) to 100\% (pure synthetic) while testing on identical real spam.

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Classifier architecture fundamentally determines robustness to synthetic training data.} Random Forest demonstrates substantially higher resilience to LLM-generated synthetic spam compared to SVM across both generation models. For GPT-4.1-mini, Random Forest maintains near-baseline performance at 100\% synthetic (F1: 0.793 → 0.788-0.827, -0.6\% to +4.3\% relative), while SVM degrades significantly (F1: 0.849 → 0.617-0.735, -13.4\% to -27.3\% relative). For Claude-3.5-Haiku original prompt, Random Forest exhibits minimal degradation (F1: 0.793 → 0.796, +0.4\%), whereas SVM suffers severe performance loss (F1: 0.849 → 0.457, -46.2\%). The ensemble architecture's inherent regularization and feature subsampling mechanisms appear to mitigate distributional shift effects between synthetic and real spam.

\textbf{(2) Precision-recall trade-offs differ fundamentally between classifier architectures.} SVM exhibits extreme precision-recall asymmetry, maintaining near-perfect precision (0.95-1.00) while recall degrades substantially (0.76 → 0.30-0.60 at 100\% synthetic), resulting in overly conservative classification. In contrast, Random Forest maintains balanced precision-recall profiles (precision: 0.67-0.85, recall: 0.78-0.98) with both metrics remaining stable under synthetic data substitution. This architectural difference suggests SVM learns overly strict decision boundaries from synthetic spam, while Random Forest's ensemble voting mechanism provides more robust generalization across the real-synthetic distribution gap.

\textbf{(3) Prompt engineering exhibits classifier-dependent effects on synthetic data quality.} For SVM, weaker prompts consistently degrade performance across both models (GPT: F1 0.735 → 0.617 from original to weak; Claude: F1 0.457 → 0.387). However, Random Forest demonstrates non-monotonic prompt sensitivity: for GPT-4.1-mini, weak prompt synthetic data yields superior performance (F1 = 0.827) compared to original (F1 = 0.788) and strong (F1 = 0.802) prompts, suggesting that reduced spam signal intensity may better match Random Forest's feature space. For Claude-3.5-Haiku, both architectures show monotonic degradation with weaker prompts, though Random Forest maintains substantially higher absolute performance (weak prompt: RF F1 = 0.637 vs SVM F1 = 0.387).

\textbf{(4) Linear degradation patterns enable predictable performance estimation.} Figure~\ref{fig:track1_gpt} and Figure~\ref{fig:track1_claude} demonstrate highly linear F1 decline as synthetic ratio increases ($R^2 > 0.95$ for SVM configurations, detailed regression analysis in Appendix~\ref{appendix:degradation_models}). This linearity enables reliable interpolation for intermediate mixing ratios and predictable performance boundaries for operational deployment. Random Forest exhibits near-zero slopes across most configurations, indicating stable performance across the full synthetic ratio spectrum.

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Classifier-dependent response to increased training data.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:track2a_claude} and Figure~\ref{fig:track2a_gpt}, SVM exhibits substantial monotonic improvement with additional real spam training data: for GPT detection, F1 increases from 0.744 (100 spam) to 0.881 (200 spam, +18.4\% relative); for Claude detection, F1 improves from 0.532 to 0.624 (+17.3\%). In contrast, Random Forest maintains stable or slightly declining performance (F1: 0.83 → 0.79 for GPT, 0.80 → 0.78 for Claude), suggesting it already achieves near-optimal feature learning at baseline training size while SVM benefits from additional training examples to refine decision boundaries.

\textbf{(2) Prompt strength inversely correlates with detectability.} Weak prompts generate the most detectable synthetic spam across both models and training sizes: for GPT at 100 spam, weak prompt yields F1 = 0.636 (SVM) vs 0.744 (original, -14.5\% relative); for Claude, the gap is more dramatic (0.335 vs 0.532, -37.0\%). Strong prompts produce intermediate detectability. This inverse relationship indicates weaker prompts generate lower-fidelity synthetic spam with greater distributional divergence from real spam, making them easier to detect but less useful for data augmentation (consistent with Track 1 findings). Original prompts produce the most deceptive synthetic spam, suggesting careful prompt design is critical for generating realistic AI spam.

\textbf{(3) Model-dependent baseline detectability reveals generation quality differences.} At baseline training (100 spam), GPT synthetic spam exhibits higher detectability (SVM: 0.636-0.744 across prompts, RF: 0.830-0.831) compared to Claude synthetic spam (SVM: 0.335-0.589, RF: 0.747-0.811). The 40-100\% relative difference in SVM baseline performance suggests GPT generates synthetic spam closer to real spam feature distributions while maintaining consistency, whereas Claude produces more divergent patterns. Interestingly, this higher detectability paradoxically correlates with GPT's superior performance in Track 1 (better training utility), suggesting distributional proximity to real spam enables both effective classifier training and effective detection.

\textbf{(4) Random Forest demonstrates superior prompt invariance.} Random Forest maintains remarkably consistent performance across all three prompt strategies for GPT detection (F1 $\approx$ 0.79-0.83 across all configurations, $<$5\% variance), while SVM shows 17-29\% variance across prompts. This robustness indicates ensemble methods effectively extract prompt-invariant features for AI-spam detection, providing more reliable defense against varied generation strategies.

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Cross-model synthetic augmentation provides substantial detection improvements.} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:track2b_claude} and Figure~\ref{fig:track2b_gpt}, training with synthetic spam from one LLM significantly improves detection of another LLM's synthetic spam. For Claude detection using GPT synthetic training data, SVM improves from baseline F1 = 0.532-0.590 to 0.688-0.860 at 100\% synthetic (+29.3\% to +45.8\% relative across prompts). For GPT detection using Claude synthetic training data, Random Forest achieves F1 = 0.894-0.908 at 100\% synthetic (+8.5\% to +10.2\% relative), demonstrating effective cross-model feature learning. This indicates LLM-generated spam shares underlying statistical patterns that generalize across different generation sources.

\textbf{(2) Prompt-dependent augmentation effectiveness reveals generation diversity impact.} Weak prompt training data provides the largest improvements for detecting strong/original prompt synthetic spam: for Claude detection, weak prompt training at 100\% synthetic achieves F1 = 0.805 (SVM), surpassing original prompt training (F1 = 0.688, +17.0\% relative). Strong prompt training data produces intermediate improvements (F1 = 0.860). This counterintuitive pattern suggests diverse, lower-quality synthetic spam (from weak prompts) better captures the full feature space of AI-generated content, enabling more robust detection across varied generation strategies. Training on higher-quality synthetic spam (strong/original prompts) may overfit to specific generation characteristics.

\textbf{(3) Random Forest exhibits remarkable cross-model robustness with prompt-invariant detection.} Random Forest maintains stable baseline performance for Claude detection across all prompts (F1 = 0.748-0.806 at 0\% synthetic, $<$8\% variance) and achieves consistent improvements with synthetic augmentation (F1 = 0.802-0.823 at 100\% for original/strong prompts). For GPT detection, Random Forest shows perfect baseline invariance (F1 = 0.824 across all prompts) and substantial improvements at 100\% synthetic (F1 = 0.834-0.908). In contrast, SVM exhibits 40-76\% baseline variance across prompts and less consistent augmentation benefits. This indicates ensemble methods effectively extract cross-model and cross-prompt invariant features, providing reliable AI-spam detection across diverse generation conditions.

\textbf{(4) Optimal synthetic ratios depend on target model and classifier architecture.} For Claude detection, all configurations show monotonic improvement with increasing synthetic ratio (0\% → 50\% → 100\%), with SVM benefiting most dramatically (+29-140\% relative). For GPT detection, Random Forest prefers high synthetic ratios (optimal at 100\%), while SVM shows non-monotonic behavior with peak performance at 50\% synthetic (F1 = 0.770-0.822) before declining at 100\% (F1 = 0.649-0.696). This suggests overfitting when SVM trains exclusively on cross-model synthetic spam, while Random Forest's ensemble nature provides regularization against such overfitting. Complete training ratio sensitivity analysis provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:training_ratio_analysis}.

\subsection{Cross-Track Synthesis}

Comparing results across all three tracks reveals fundamental patterns in LLM-generated spam effectiveness:

\textbf{Asymmetric offense-defense dynamics.} Track 1 shows GPT synthetic data maintains 86.6\% effectiveness for training (attack utility), while Track 2a shows 74.4\% baseline detection (defensive readiness). Claude shows opposite pattern: 53.8\% training effectiveness but only 53.2\% baseline detectability. This asymmetry indicates model selection critically determines whether generation quality favors offensive training augmentation or defensive evasion capabilities.

\textbf{Architectural robustness advantages.} Random Forest consistently outperforms SVM across all tracks (mean improvement: +15.3\% in Track 1 Claude, +27.1\% in Track 2a Claude baseline, stable high performance in Track 2b), demonstrating ensemble methods provide superior robustness against distributional shifts between real and synthetic spam, and between cross-model synthetic sources.

\textbf{Cross-model feature transferability.} Track 2b demonstrates that synthetic augmentation from one LLM improves detection of another LLM's spam (+30\% for Claude detection, +8.5\% for GPT detection with Random Forest), indicating LLM-generated spam shares underlying feature patterns that generalize across generation sources despite model-specific variations.

Complete statistical analysis including hypothesis tests, effect sizes, confidence intervals, and detailed performance metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, AUC-ROC) are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:detailed_statistics}.
