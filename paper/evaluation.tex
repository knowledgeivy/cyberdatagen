\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Our comprehensive evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness and limitations of synthetic spam data generation across multiple methodologies through systematic analysis of 6,160 experimental configurations. We compare two state-of-the-art LLM-based approaches (GPT-4.1-mini and Claude-3.5-Haiku) against a traditional feature-space augmentation baseline (SMOTE), evaluating 132 unique LLM configurations and 44 SMOTE configurations, each replicated across 20 independent groups. The results reveal profound differences between text-space and feature-space augmentation paradigms, with performance patterns depending critically on classifier architecture, synthetic data proportion, mixing strategy, and generation methodology. Through rigorous statistical analysis incorporating confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, and sensitivity metrics, we establish quantitative relationships between synthetic data characteristics and practical utility for spam detection.

The results reveal striking contrasts across augmentation paradigms and classifier architectures:

\textbf{Random Forest demonstrates exceptional robustness} across all methods, maintaining 97-98\% of baseline performance with LLM-generated data. Remarkably, SMOTE-augmented training \emph{improves} Random Forest performance by 6.4\%, achieving F1 = 0.858 compared to baseline 0.807. This improvement aligns with Random Forest's ability to effectively partition feature space enhanced by SMOTE's convex-hull interpolation generating samples within existing feature distributions.

\textbf{SVM exhibits severe method-dependent sensitivity}, with degradation varying dramatically: SMOTE causes minimal degradation (-5.7\%), GPT-4.1-mini moderate degradation (-24.1\%), and Claude-3.5-Haiku catastrophic degradation (-46.0\%). This pattern suggests margin-based classifiers are highly vulnerable to distributional shifts in text-space generation, where LLM-introduced linguistic variations may violate SVM's geometric assumptions more severely than feature-space interpolation.

\textbf{LLM model selection critically impacts SVM performance}, with Claude-3.5-Haiku underperforming GPT-4.1-mini by 21.9 percentage points at 100\% synthetic. In contrast, Random Forest shows near-equivalent robustness to both LLM models (-2.1\% vs -1.6\%), suggesting ensemble methods effectively aggregate information regardless of text generation quality. The substantially higher baseline of Random Forest (F1 = 0.793-0.807 vs. 0.627-0.676 for SVM) persists across all conditions, maintaining consistent advantages of 0.15-0.22 F1 points.

\textbf{SMOTE baseline superiority} for SVM is particularly noteworthy: feature-space interpolation outperforms both LLM approaches by 18-40 percentage points in degradation mitigation. However, SMOTE's lack of text interpretability and inability to generate novel semantic patterns limits applicability to adversarial testing and human review scenarios where LLMs provide unique value despite lower classification performance.

The regression analysis quantifies the stark method-classifier interactions. The mixing strategy comparison reveals three key patterns:

\textbf{LLM methods show classifier-dependent strategy effects}. For both GPT and Claude, Random Forest achieves slightly better performance with cross-group mixing (+0.011 to +0.015 F1), suggesting ensemble methods benefit from the additional diversity introduced by out-of-distribution synthetic samples. Conversely, SVM shows mixed patterns: GPT achieves identical performance across strategies (0.476 vs. 0.476), while Claude shows modest cross-group improvement (0.353 vs. 0.338, +0.015). This pattern suggests feature-space interpolation benefits from cross-group diversity more substantially than text-space generation, possibly because SMOTE's convex-hull sampling naturally generalizes across feature distributions without semantic drift concerns that affect LLM-generated text.

\textbf{Practical implications}: The near-equivalent performance across mixing strategies for LLM methods simplifies deployment—practitioners can use either within-group (simpler, matches target distribution) or cross-group (more generalizable, useful when target groups have insufficient samples) without significant performance penalties. SMOTE's preference for cross-group mixing suggests practitioners should prioritize diversity over precise distribution matching when using feature-space augmentation methods.

The results reveal striking model-dependent sensitivity to prompt engineering:

\textbf{GPT-4.1-mini demonstrates prompt invariance}, with performance variation ranges of only 0.003 for SVM and 0.002 for Random Forest across all three strategies. This robustness suggests GPT's generation process maintains consistent spam characteristics regardless of prompt instruction intensity, possibly due to strong pre-training on similar content or inherent generation stability.

\textbf{Claude-3.5-Haiku exhibits significant prompt sensitivity}, particularly for weak prompts. Weak prompt degrades SVM performance to 0.496 (10.8\% worse than original, 21\% below baseline), while strong and original prompts perform comparably (0.543-0.550). Random Forest also shows degradation patterns across prompts: original (0.779) $>$ strong (0.768) $>$ weak (0.752), spanning 0.027 F1 points. \textbf{Practical implications}: For GPT-based augmentation, practitioners can use any prompt strategy without performance concerns, simplifying deployment. For Claude-based systems, original or strong prompts are essential to maintain acceptable performance, with weak prompts causing unacceptable degradation. When classification performance is paramount and text interpretability is not required, SMOTE remains the superior choice regardless of synthetic proportion.

\subsection{Statistical Significance and Hypothesis Testing}

To rigorously assess performance differences, we conduct paired t-tests comparing each synthetic ratio against baseline (0\% synthetic) and cross-method comparisons. We apply Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction ($\alpha = 0.05$) across all comparisons.

\subsubsection{LLM-Based Degradation Significance}

For GPT-4.1-mini SVM (original prompt, within-group):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{10\%}: $\Delta = -0.009$, $p < 10^{-10}$, Cohen's $d = 0.35$ (small-medium)
    \item \textbf{50\%}: $\Delta = -0.062$, $p < 10^{-35}$, Cohen's $d = 2.02$ (very large)
    \item \textbf{100\%}: $\Delta = -0.151$, $p < 10^{-40}$, Cohen's $d = 4.12$ (extremely large)
\end{itemize}

Claude-3.5-Haiku exhibits even more severe degradation for SVM:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{10\%}: $\Delta = -0.015$, $p < 10^{-12}$, Cohen's $d = 0.58$ (medium)
    \item \textbf{50\%}: $\Delta = -0.077$, $p < 10^{-38}$, Cohen's $d = 2.89$ (extremely large)
    \item \textbf{100\%}: $\Delta = -0.289$, $p < 10^{-45}$, Cohen's $d = 9.86$ (catastrophic)
\end{itemize}

All LLM synthetic ratios $\geq$10\% show highly significant SVM degradation ($p < 0.001$) with monotonically increasing effect sizes. For Random Forest, both LLMs maintain non-significant or small effects ($d < 0.5$) across all ratios, with Claude showing slightly better robustness than GPT (-1.6\% vs -2.1\% at 100\%).

\subsubsection{SMOTE Performance Significance}

SMOTE demonstrates fundamentally different significance patterns. For Random Forest at 100\% synthetic:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SMOTE vs. Baseline}: $\Delta = +0.051$, $p = 3.4\times10^{-8}$, Cohen's $d = 0.78$ (medium-large \emph{improvement})
    \item \textbf{SMOTE vs. GPT}: $\Delta = +0.081$, $p < 10^{-15}$, Cohen's $d = 1.98$ (very large advantage)
    \item \textbf{SMOTE vs. Claude}: $\Delta = +0.077$, $p < 10^{-14}$, Cohen's $d = 1.62$ (very large advantage)
\end{itemize}

For SVM, SMOTE shows non-significant degradation at moderate ratios ($d < 0.2$ for $\leq$50\% synthetic) and only small degradation at 100\% ($d = 0.39$), significantly outperforming both LLMs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SMOTE vs. GPT} (100\%): $\Delta = +0.161$, $p < 10^{-28}$, Cohen's $d = 3.87$
    \item \textbf{SMOTE vs. Claude} (100\%): $\Delta = +0.299$, $p < 10^{-42}$, Cohen's $d = 8.94$
\end{itemize}

These comparisons establish SMOTE's statistically significant and practically meaningful superiority for classification performance, with effect sizes exceeding conventional "large" thresholds by substantial margins.

\subsection{Summary of Key Findings}

Our comprehensive evaluation across 6,160 experimental configurations establishes five principal conclusions:

\textbf{(1) Method-Classifier Interaction Dominates Performance}: SMOTE achieves superior classification performance for SVM (-5.7\% degradation vs -24\% for GPT, -46\% for Claude) and improves Random Forest performance (+6.4\%), while LLMs provide unique interpretability benefits despite lower raw performance.

\textbf{(2) Dramatic Architectural Dependence}: Random Forest demonstrates 4-40$\times$ greater robustness than SVM across all methods, with method selection more critical for margin-based classifiers than ensemble methods.

\textbf{(3) Model-Specific Prompt Sensitivity}: GPT exhibits prompt invariance (0.003 F1 range), while Claude shows significant sensitivity (0.054 F1 range), indicating practitioners must carefully tune prompts for Claude but not GPT deployments.

\textbf{(4) Distribution Matching Flexibility}: Within-group and cross-group strategies show near-equivalent LLM performance, with SMOTE demonstrating modest cross-group advantages, simplifying practical deployment decisions.

\textbf{(5) Feature-Space vs Text-Space Trade-offs}: SMOTE's superior classification performance comes at the cost of semantic interpretability, while LLMs' human-readable outputs enable adversarial testing and review despite 18-40 percentage point higher degradation for SVM.

These findings establish that augmentation method selection should prioritize application requirements: choose SMOTE for maximum classification performance with Random Forest, or LLMs when text interpretability is essential for downstream security analysis, human review, or adversarial testing scenarios.
