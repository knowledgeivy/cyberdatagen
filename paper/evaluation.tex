\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Our evaluation systematically assesses LLM-generated synthetic spam across three complementary experimental tracks spanning 2,280 training-evaluation units (2 models × 3 prompts × multiple configurations × 20 replications). Following the methodology framework (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), we present results organized by research questions: Track 1 evaluates synthetic data for classifier training (RQ1), Track 2a establishes baseline detection capabilities against AI-powered spam (RQ2a), and Track 2b investigates hybrid training strategies (RQ2b). All experiments employ cross-group mixing strategy based on preliminary analysis showing consistent advantages over within-group mixing (detailed comparison in Appendix~\ref{appendix:mixing_strategy_comparison}).

\subsection{Track 1: Synthetic-to-Real Detection}
\label{sec:eval:track1}

This track addresses RQ1: \textit{Can LLM-generated synthetic spam effectively train classifiers to detect real spam?} We systematically vary synthetic proportion $\theta$ from 0\% (pure real) to 100\% (pure synthetic) while testing on identical real spam.

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Moderate degradation with model-dependent severity.} GPT-4.1-mini maintains 86.6\% of baseline performance at 100\% synthetic (F1 = 0.735), while Claude-3.5-Haiku drops to 53.8\% (F1 = 0.457). The 3.5× difference in degradation severity (13.4\% vs 46.2\%) indicates GPT generates synthetic spam with substantially better distributional alignment to real spam.

\textbf{(2) Prompt invariance for GPT, sensitivity for Claude.} GPT-4.1-mini shows negligible variation across all prompt strategies ($<$3\% difference), simplifying deployment. Claude-3.5-Haiku exhibits 15-25\% performance variation, with weak prompts producing significantly degraded synthetic data.

\textbf{(3) Highly linear degradation patterns.} All configurations exhibit $R^2 > 0.95$ in linear regression modeling performance vs. synthetic ratio (detailed regression analysis in Appendix~\ref{appendix:degradation_models}). This linearity enables reliable interpolation for intermediate mixing ratios.

\label{sec:eval:track2a}

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Strong zero-shot detection capabilities.} Classifiers trained solely on real spam achieve F1 = 0.532-0.824 (mean 0.726 across all configurations) when detecting AI-generated spam, indicating substantial distributional overlap between real and synthetic spam enables transfer learning without explicit AI-spam training exposure.

\textbf{(2) Model-dependent detectability asymmetry.} GPT synthetic spam exhibits higher baseline detectability (SVM: 0.744, RF: 0.824) compared to Claude synthetic spam (SVM: 0.532, RF: 0.802). The 40\% relative difference in SVM performance (0.744 vs 0.532) suggests GPT generates synthetic spam closer to real spam feature distributions, while Claude produces more divergent patterns.

\textbf{(3) Random Forest demonstrates superior robustness.} Random Forest maintains F1 $\approx$ 0.80-0.82 across both target models with minimal variation ($\pm$0.02), while SVM shows 40\% performance range (0.532-0.744). Ensemble methods appear more robust to distributional shifts between real training data and AI-generated test data.

\textbf{Key Findings:}

\textbf{(1) Differential hybrid training benefits.} For Claude detection, cross-model synthetic augmentation provides substantial improvements (SVM: +30\% relative at 100\%). For GPT detection, benefits are classifier-dependent: SVM peaks at 50\% (+3.5\%) before declining, while Random Forest improves monotonically (+8.5\% at 100\%).

\textbf{(2) Random Forest exhibits superior cross-model generalization.} Random Forest achieves F1 = 0.80-0.89 across all configurations with remarkable stability for Claude detection (perfect invariance at F1 = 0.802) and improvement for GPT detection (0.824 → 0.894). This robustness indicates ensemble methods effectively extract cross-model invariant features.

\textbf{(3) Optimal training ratios are target-model dependent.} Detecting Claude spam benefits from higher synthetic ratios (monotonic improvement), while detecting GPT spam shows optimal performance at intermediate ratios for SVM (50\%) or high ratios for Random Forest (100\%). Complete training ratio sensitivity analysis provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:training_ratio_analysis}.

\subsection{Cross-Track Synthesis}

Comparing results across all three tracks reveals fundamental patterns in LLM-generated spam effectiveness:

\textbf{Asymmetric offense-defense dynamics.} Track 1 shows GPT synthetic data maintains 86.6\% effectiveness for training (attack utility), while Track 2a shows 74.4\% baseline detection (defensive readiness). Claude shows opposite pattern: 53.8\% training effectiveness but only 53.2\% baseline detectability. This asymmetry indicates model selection critically determines whether generation quality favors offensive training augmentation or defensive evasion capabilities.

\textbf{Architectural robustness advantages.} Random Forest consistently outperforms SVM across all tracks (mean improvement: +15.3\% in Track 1 Claude, +27.1\% in Track 2a Claude baseline, stable high performance in Track 2b), demonstrating ensemble methods provide superior robustness against distributional shifts between real and synthetic spam, and between cross-model synthetic sources.

\textbf{Cross-model feature transferability.} Track 2b demonstrates that synthetic augmentation from one LLM improves detection of another LLM's spam (+30\% for Claude detection, +8.5\% for GPT detection with Random Forest), indicating LLM-generated spam shares underlying feature patterns that generalize across generation sources despite model-specific variations.

Complete statistical analysis including hypothesis tests, effect sizes, confidence intervals, and detailed performance metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, AUC-ROC) are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:detailed_statistics}.
